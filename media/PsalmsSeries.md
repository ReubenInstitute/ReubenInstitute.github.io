# Psalms Series

The **Psalms Series** publishes individual chapters of the *Book of Psalms*.

Each chapter is set to a background song generated with **Suno**, accompanied by video segments created with **Midjourney**. A separate raw version (without music or background imagery) is also published.

**Audio foundation:** The narration is based on Abraham Shmuelov’s historic Leningrad Codex readings, refined with AI voice cloning for clarity while retaining the original Sephardic pronunciation.

**Distribution:** YouTube, YouTube Shorts, Instagram, TikTok, Spotify (audio-only), WhatsApp Channel, and the IVR phone line.

## Releases

### Psalm 8

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm008.jpg)

A psalm by King David, opening and closing with wonder at the greatness of God's name across the whole earth. At its center stands the question of why God takes notice of humanity at all — and the astonishing answer that he has placed humans just below the divine, crowning them with glory and giving them dominion over all creation.

### Psalm 16

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm016.jpg)

A Michtam by David, a quiet confession of trust: he has no good apart from God, who is his allotted portion and cup. Even the body rests secure, for God will not abandon his soul to the grave nor let his faithful one see decay. The path of life leads into God’s presence, where there is fullness of joy and pleasures forevermore.

### Psalm 32

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm032.jpg)

A Maskil (a “teaching” poem, from “sekhel” — wisdom) by King David, opening with the declaration that the person whose wrongdoing is forgiven is truly fortunate. David describes the physical toll of staying silent about his sins, and the relief that came when he confessed them openly. The psalm closes with an invitation to everyone to trust in God— promising that those who do will be surrounded by kindness.

### Psalm 35

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm035.jpg)

A psalm by David, a fierce plea for God to rise as a warrior against his enemies—grasping shield and spear, driving them back like chaff before the wind. Yet the betrayal is not from strangers but from those he once mourned for as a brother; they repay his kindness with malice. The psalm pivots from accusation to confident praise, closing with a promise that his tongue will recount God’s justice all day long.

### Psalm 41

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm041.jpg)

A psalm by David, opening with a blessing on those who care for the weak—God will protect and sustain them on their sickbed. But David himself lies ill, surrounded not by comfort but by enemies who whisper of his death and a trusted friend who shared his table and then turned against him. The psalm closes with the certainty that God upholds the innocent, followed by the doxology that seals the first book of Psalms: Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting, Amen and Amen.

### Psalm 42

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm042.jpg)

A Maskil by the sons of Korah, the soul’s desperate thirst for God like a deer panting for water. Memories of leading the festive procession to the Temple only deepen the present anguish, as enemies taunt, “Where is your God?” A refrain of hope breaks through the despair, urging the downcast soul to wait for God, its savior and its light.

### Psalm 44

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm044.jpg)

A Maskil (a “teaching” poem, from “sekhel” — wisdom) by the sons of Korah, opening with a proud recounting of how God gave their ancestors victory— not by their own strength but by his. The mood shifts sharply into bewilderment and anguish: God seems to have abandoned them, scattering them among the goyim despite their faithfulness. The psalm ends with a desperate cry— wake up, God, and redeem us.

### Psalm 46

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm046.jpg)

A song by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), declaring that God is a refuge and a source of strength no matter what collapses around them— mountains, kingdoms, the earth itself. At its center is a vision of God's city, secure and unshakeable, while chaos reigns everywhere else. The psalm closes with God's own voice commanding stillness: "let go, and know that I am God."

### Psalm 47

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm047.jpg)

A psalm by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), bursting with jubilant calls for all peoples to clap their hands and shout to God. It celebrates God as a great king over the whole earth who has subdued all the nations. The psalm reaches its climax with God ascending his holy throne amid trumpet blasts and singing, as the nobles of all nations gather as the people of Abraham's God.

### Psalm 59

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm059.jpg)

A Michtam by David, written when Saul sent men to watch his house and kill him. Fierce enemies prowl like dogs, snarling and circling the city, but David’s refuge is God himself. The refrain returns twice: in the evening the dogs return, but in the morning David sings of God’s strength, for God is his fortress and the one who shows him steadfast love.

### Psalm 75

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm075.jpg)

A psalm by Asaf (Asaph, King David's chief musician), opening with thanksgiving before God himself speaks— declaring that he alone sets the time of judgment and holds the world steady. The arrogant are warned not to boast, since it is God who raises one person and brings another down. The psalm ends with the promise that the power of the wicked will be broken, and the righteous will rise.

### Psalm 77

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm077.jpg)

A psalm by Asaf, a cry into the night that refuses comfort. The memory of better days only sharpens the pain: has God’s mercy vanished forever? The turning point comes not from an answer but from remembering the mighty deeds of God—the waters parting, the earth trembling, the people led like a flock by Moses and Aaron. The past becomes the anchor for a shaken faith.

### Psalm 84

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm084.jpg)

A song by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), overflowing with longing for God's dwelling place— so intense that even a bird nesting near the altar is envied. The pilgrim's journey toward Zion is described as one that transforms hardship into blessing, moving from strength to strength. The psalm closes with the declaration that a single day in God's presence is worth more than a thousand anywhere else.

### Psalm 86

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm086.jpg)

A prayer by David, bowing low as a poor and needy servant, yet confident that God is good, forgiving, and incomparable among all powers. He asks for a single, undivided heart to revere God’s name, and praises the mercy that pulled him from the lowest pit. The psalm closes with a call for a sign of favor— one that will shame his enemies and bring him the comfort that only God can give.

### Psalm 90

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm090.jpg)

A prayer by Moses (Moshe), the only psalm attributed to him, contrasting God's eternity with the brevity and fragility of human life. A thousand years are like a single day to God— while human beings sprout and wither like grass. The psalm ends not in despair but in petition: teach us to count our days wisely, and let the work of our hands endure.

### Psalm 93

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm093.jpg)

A short, powerful declaration that God is king— clothed in majesty and strength, his throne established before time itself. The roaring of the seas, mighty as they are, cannot match the power of God on high. The psalm closes with the quiet certainty that God's decrees are trustworthy and his house is holy, forever.

### Psalm 99

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm099.jpg)

A royal proclamation: God reigns, enthroned upon the cherubim — let the nations tremble and the earth quake. He is mighty in Zion, exalted above all peoples, a king whose strength is married to justice. The psalm recalls Moses, Aaron, and Samuel as voices who called on Him and were answered, forgiven, yet held to account. It closes with a double summons to exalt the Lord and bow before His holy mountain, for He alone is holy.

### Psalm 105

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm105.jpg)

A historical psalm, a sweeping recollection of God’s covenant faithfulness from Abraham to Moses. It recounts the promises to the patriarchs, the sojourn in Egypt, the sending of Moses and Aaron, the plagues, and the exodus, all as evidence of God’s unwavering commitment to His people. The psalm ends with the reminder that all this was done so that they might keep His statutes and observe His laws. Hallelu Yah!

### Psalm 117

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm117.jpg)

The shortest psalm in the entire collection— a two-verse call for all the goyim (foreign peoples) to praise God, grounded in a single reason: his kindness toward Israel has prevailed, and his faithfulness endures forever.

### Psalm 129

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm129.jpg)

A Pilgrim Song (Shir HaMa'alot, sung on the ascent to the Temple in Jerusalem), voiced as Israel looking back on a lifetime of persecution that never succeeded in destroying them. The image of plowers cutting long furrows across a back captures the depth of the suffering— yet God cut their cords. The psalm ends not with celebration but with a quiet, determined wish that all who hate Zion will wither like rooftop grass that no one bothers to harvest.

### Psalm 130

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm130.jpg)

A Pilgrim Song (Shir HaMa’alot), a cry from the absolute depths — not a request for help against enemies, but the soul’s call to God in the face of its own wrongdoing. Yet the psalm does not remain in the abyss: forgiveness belongs to God, and the soul waits for Him more urgently than watchmen wait for dawn. The final verses open outward, calling all Israel to hope in a God who will redeem them from every sin.

### Psalm 137

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm137.jpg)

By the rivers of Babylon, the exiles sat and wept, hanging their harps on the willows. Their captors demanded songs of Zion, but how could they sing the Lord’s song in a foreign land? The psalm moves from sorrow to an unshakeable oath of loyalty to Jerusalem, closing with a stark cry for justice that refuses to forget the day of Jerusalem’s fall.

### Psalm 150

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm150.jpg)

The final psalm, a resounding crescendo of praise. Every instrument— trumpet, lute, harp, drum, strings, pipe, and crashing cymbals— is summoned to sound God’s glory. Everything that breathes must praise the Lord. A perfect, ecstatic close to the entire Psalter: Hallelu Yah!
