# Psalms Series

The **Psalms Series** publishes individual chapters of the *Book of Psalms*.

Each chapter is set to a background song generated with **Suno**, accompanied by video segments created with **Midjourney**. A separate raw version (without music or background imagery) is also published.

**Audio foundation:** The narration is based on Abraham Shmuelov’s historic Leningrad Codex readings, refined with AI voice cloning for clarity while retaining the original Sephardic pronunciation.

**Distribution:** YouTube, YouTube Shorts, Instagram, TikTok, Spotify (audio-only), WhatsApp Channel, and the IVR phone line.




## Releases

### Psalm 1

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm001.jpg)

Two ways of living are placed before a person: one that takes root in delighting in God's instruction and yields stability and fruitfulness, and another that rejects that wisdom and ends in emptiness. The first is compared to a tree that grows near water, the second to chaff — the husk of the grain — blown away by the wind.

### Psalm 2

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm002.jpg)

Foreign rulers plot rebellion against God and his anointed king, only to be met with divine laughter and the declaration that the king has already been installed on Zion. The king reveals that God has adopted him as a son and will give him dominion over the nations, then warns the rebellious to submit and take refuge in him before his anger flares — but promises safety to all who do.

### Psalm 3

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm003.jpg)

A psalm by King David, written as he flees from his son Absalom (Avshalom). Surrounded by enemies who claim God has abandoned him, David responds not with panic but with calm confidence that God is his shield and the one who answers when he calls. Despite the danger, he sleeps peacefully and wakes strengthened, then asks God to deliver him and declares that salvation belongs to God alone.

### Psalm 4

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm004.jpg)

King David confronts those who insult him and chase after lies, urging them to search their hearts and turn to God. He finds deeper joy in God's presence than they find in material gain, and he lies down to sleep in peace, knowing God alone keeps him safe.

### Psalm 5

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm005.jpg)

King David calls out to God with his troubles, describing him as one who cannot tolerate evil, and asks him to expose and condemn the enemies who surround him. The psalm closes with the assurance that those who trust in God will be protected and filled with joy.

### Psalm 6

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm006.jpg)

A psalm by King David, crying out from a place of physical and emotional collapse, begging God not to punish him in anger but to heal and rescue him — for the dead cannot praise God. The psalm turns sharply when David declares that God has heard his weeping, and ends with his enemies routed in shame.

### Psalm 7

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm007.jpg)

A Shiggayon (a "wandering" poem, from "shagah" — to stray) by King David, sung to God in response to the accusations of Kush ben Yemini (a Benjaminite). David protests his innocence and calls on God to judge him and strike down the wicked, describing evil as a trap that collapses on the one who digs it. The psalm closes with confident praise for God's justice.

### Psalm 8

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm008.jpg)

A psalm by King David, opening and closing with wonder at the greatness of God's name across the whole earth. At its center stands the question of why God takes notice of humanity at all — and the astonishing answer that he has placed humans just below the divine, crowning them with glory and giving them dominion over all creation.

### Psalm 10

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm010.jpg)

An anguished cry asking why God stands at a distance while the wicked prey on the vulnerable — describing the ruthless arrogance of evil people who act as though God neither sees nor cares. The psalm turns into a direct plea for God to intervene, and ends with trust that he does see, does hear, and will bring justice to the orphan and the oppressed.

### Psalm 16

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm016.jpg)

A Michtam (a type of psalm, probably meaning "a golden poem" — from "ketem", gold) by King David, a quiet confession of trust: he has no good apart from God, who is his chosen portion and his source of security. Even his body rests secure, for God will not abandon him to the grave nor let his faithful one see decay. The path of life leads into God's presence, where there is fullness of joy and pleasures forevermore.

### Psalm 32

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm032.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel" — wisdom) by King David, opening with the declaration that the person whose wrongdoing is forgiven is truly fortunate. David describes the physical toll of staying silent about his sins, and the relief that came when he confessed them openly. The psalm closes with an invitation to everyone to trust in God— promising that those who do will be surrounded by kindness.

### Psalm 35

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm035.jpg)

A psalm by King David, a fierce plea for God to rise as a warrior against his enemies—grasping shield and spear, driving them back like chaff before the wind. Yet the betrayal is not from strangers but from those he once mourned for as a brother; they repay his kindness with malice. The psalm pivots from accusation to confident praise, closing with a promise that his tongue will recount God's justice all day long.

### Psalm 41

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm041.jpg)

A psalm by King David, opening with a blessing on those who care for the weak—God will protect and sustain them when they are ill. But David himself lies sick, surrounded not by comfort but by enemies who whisper about his death and a close friend who shared his table and then betrayed him. The psalm ends with the certainty that God upholds the innocent, and then comes a blessing praising God as the eternal God of Israel — the blessing that completes the first book of Psalms — with the words "Amen and Amen."

### Psalm 42

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm042.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel" — wisdom) by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), the soul's desperate thirst for God like a deer panting for water. Memories of leading the festive procession to the Temple only deepen the present anguish, as enemies taunt him with the question of where his God is. A refrain of hope breaks through the despair, urging the downcast soul to wait for God, its savior and its light.

### Psalm 44

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm044.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel" — wisdom) by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), opening with a proud recounting of how God gave their ancestors victory— not by their own strength but by his. The mood shifts sharply into bewilderment and anguish: God seems to have abandoned them, scattering them among the goyim (foreign peoples) despite their faithfulness. The psalm ends with a desperate cry for God to rouse himself and save them.

### Psalm 46

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm046.jpg)

A song by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), declaring that God is a refuge and a source of strength no matter what collapses around them— mountains, kingdoms, the earth itself. At its center is a vision of God's city, secure and unshakeable, while chaos reigns everywhere else. The psalm closes with God's own voice calling for stillness and the recognition that he is God.

### Psalm 47

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm047.jpg)

A psalm by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), bursting with jubilant calls for all peoples to clap their hands and shout to God. It celebrates God as a great king over the whole earth who has subdued all the nations. The psalm reaches its climax with God ascending his holy throne amid trumpet blasts and singing, as the nobles of all nations gather as the people of Abraham's God.

### Psalm 48

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm048.jpg)

A song by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), celebrating Jerusalem as the city of God, beautiful in its height and the joy of the whole earth. It describes how kings gathered against it but were seized with fear and fled like ships shattered by an east wind. The psalm then turns to the experience of God's people inside the city, remembering his kindness in the temple and praising him for his justice. It ends with a call to walk around Zion, count its towers and walls, and tell the next generation that this God is our God forever, who will guide us always.

### Psalm 52

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm052.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel" — wisdom) by King David, written after Doeg the Edomite told King Saul that David had visited the priest Ahimelech. The psalm confronts a powerful man who boasts in evil and uses his words like a sharp blade to harm others, loving lies more than truth. God will tear him down forever, uprooting him from his home and from the land of the living. The righteous will see this and be reminded not to trust in wealth or destruction, but to be like David, who describes himself as a green olive tree in God's house, trusting in God's enduring kindness and praising him forever.

### Psalm 53

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm053.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel"—wisdom) by King David, set to Mahalath (a musical term of uncertain meaning). It closely echoes Psalm 14: the fool says in his heart there is no God, and corruption spreads among people, with no one doing good. God looks down from heaven to see if anyone seeks wisdom, but all have turned aside. The psalm describes the sudden terror of the wicked, for God scatters the bones of those who attack his people, and it closes with a longing for salvation to come from Zion so that Jacob may rejoice and Israel be glad.

### Psalm 54

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm054.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel"—wisdom) by King David, written when the Ziphites told Saul that David was hiding among them. He asks God to save him by his name and vindicate him by his power, for strangers and ruthless people have risen against him, people who do not set God before them. The psalm turns to confidence: God is his helper and the one who upholds his life. It closes with a vow to offer a freewill sacrifice and praise God's name, for he has delivered him from every trouble.

### Psalm 56

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm056.jpg)

A Michtam (a type of psalm, probably meaning "a golden poem"—from "ketem", gold) by King David, written when the Philistines seized him in Gath. It opens with a plea for mercy because enemies press him all day long, but the refrain returns again and again: when he is afraid, he trusts in God, and with God on his side he asks what mere flesh can do to him. The psalm closes with gratitude because God has kept his feet from stumbling and delivered his soul from death, so he may walk before God in the light of life.

### Psalm 59

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm059.jpg)

A Michtam (a type of psalm, probably meaning "a golden poem" — from "ketem", gold) by King David, written when King Saul (Shaul) sent men to watch his house and kill him. Fierce enemies prowl like dogs, snarling and circling the city, but David's refuge is God himself. The refrain returns twice: in the evening the dogs return, but in the morning David sings of God's strength, for God is his fortress and the one who shows him steadfast love.

### Psalm 60

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm060.jpg)

A Michtam by King David, written for teaching against the background of his wars with Aram and Edom. It begins with distress as though God has rejected his people, but turns to God's promises, a plea for help, and confidence that victory cannot come from human strength alone.

### Psalm 61

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm061.jpg)

A psalm by King David, a prayer from a distant place, when his heart is overwhelmed. He asks God to hear his cry and lead him to a safe, high rock, for God has been his refuge and strong tower. He longs to dwell in God's tent forever and take shelter under his wings, trusting that God has heard his vows and given him the inheritance of those who honor his name. He asks for long life for the king and declares that he will sing God's name forever and keep his promises daily.

### Psalm 66

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm066.jpg)

A song of praise, calling all the earth to shout joyfully to God and to recognize his awe-inspiring deeds, including the parting of the sea and the crossing of the river on foot. The psalm then shifts to personal testimony: God has tested his people through hardship and danger, but he has kept them alive and brought them out to a place of fullness. The speaker promises to bring offerings in gratitude and invites everyone who fears God to hear how God answered prayer and did not withhold his kindness.

### Psalm 67

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm067.jpg)

A psalm asking God to be gracious and bless his people, to make his face shine upon them, so that his way may be known on earth and his saving power among all nations. It calls on all peoples to praise God, for he judges fairly and guides the nations of the earth. The psalm closes with the image of the land yielding its harvest and God blessing his people, so that all the ends of the earth will revere him.

### Psalm 70

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm070.jpg)

A psalm by King David, marked as a poem "to bring to remembrance" — perhaps a request that God remember and act. It is an urgent, brief cry for help: he asks God to hurry to rescue him, and for those who seek his life to be turned back in shame. At the center he contrasts the enemies with those who seek God — they will rejoice and say that God is great. David closes by describing himself as poor and in need, and once more begs God not to delay.

### Psalm 74

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm074.jpg)

A Maskil (a "teaching" poem, from "sekhel"—wisdom) by Asaf (Asaph, King David's chief musician), a lament over the destruction of the temple, describing how enemies have invaded the sanctuary, smashed its carved wood, set it on fire, and mocked the name of God. The psalm asks how long the foe will be allowed to scoff, then turns to remember God's ancient power—breaking the sea, crushing the heads of sea monsters, establishing day and night and the seasons. It closes with a plea for God to remember his covenant, rise up and defend his cause, and not forget the oppressed and needy who praise his name.

### Psalm 75

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm075.jpg)

A psalm by Asaf (Asaph, King David's chief musician), opening with thanksgiving before God himself speaks— declaring that he alone sets the time of judgment and holds the world steady. The arrogant are warned not to boast, since it is God who raises one person and brings another down. The psalm ends with the promise that the power of the wicked will be broken, and the righteous will rise.

### Psalm 77

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm077.jpg)

A psalm by Asaf (Asaph, King David's chief musician), a cry into the night that refuses comfort. The memory of better days only sharpens the pain: he wonders whether God's mercy has disappeared forever. The turning point comes not from an answer but from remembering the mighty deeds of God—the waters parting, the earth trembling, the people led like a flock by Moses and Aaron. The past becomes the anchor for a shaken faith.

### Psalm 84

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm084.jpg)

A song by the sons of Korah (Korach, from the tribe of Levi, whose sons became temple musicians), overflowing with longing for God's dwelling place— so intense that even a bird nesting near the altar is envied. The pilgrim's journey toward Zion is described as one that transforms hardship into blessing, moving from strength to strength. The psalm closes with the declaration that a single day in God's presence is worth more than a thousand anywhere else.

### Psalm 86

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm086.jpg)

A prayer by King David, humbling himself as someone poor and in need, yet confident that God is good, forgiving, and greater than all other powers. He asks for a whole and focused heart to honor God's name, and praises the mercy that rescued him from deep suffering. The psalm closes with a request for a clear sign of God's favor—one that will put his enemies to shame and bring him the comfort that only God can give.

### Psalm 90

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm090.jpg)

A prayer by Moses (Moshe), the only psalm attributed to him, placing God's eternity side by side with the shortness and fragility of human life. To God, a thousand years are like a single day—while human beings sprout and wither like grass. The psalm ends not in despair but in a request: teach us to count our days wisely, and let the work of our hands have lasting value.

### Psalm 91

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm091.jpg)

A psalm of protection, describing the safety of the one who dwells in God's shelter: he is shielded from hidden traps, disease, and dangers that strike by night and by day. Though thousands fall around him, harm will not reach him, because God commands his angels to guard him wherever he goes and to keep him from stumbling. He will walk safely over dangerous beasts. The psalm closes with God speaking directly, promising to rescue, protect, and grant long life to the one who loves him and calls on his name.

### Psalm 93

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm093.jpg)

A short, powerful declaration that God is king— clothed in majesty and strength, his throne established before time itself. The roaring of the seas, mighty as they are, cannot match the power of God on high. The psalm closes with the quiet certainty that God's decrees are trustworthy and his house is holy, forever.

### Psalm 99

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm099.jpg)

A royal psalm celebrating God as king — not just powerful, but just. It describes him as enthroned above the cherubim (heavenly beings), and says that his rule should make the nations tremble. The psalm then recalls Moses, Aaron, and Samuel as examples of people who spoke with God and were answered — forgiven when they went wrong, but also held responsible for their actions. It closes with a call to worship God at his holy mountain, because he is holy.

### Psalm 101

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm101.jpg)

A psalm by King David, a personal vow to run his own house and life with integrity. He resolves to keep wickedness, slander, arrogance, and deceit far from him, and to surround himself instead with those who are honest and faithful. The psalm closes with his determination to silence every evildoer, clearing wrongdoers each morning from the city of God.

### Psalm 105

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm105.jpg)

A historical psalm that recounts God's faithfulness to his covenant from Abraham to Moses. It describes the promises to the ancestors, the time spent in Egypt, the sending of Moses and Aaron, the plagues, and the exodus — all as evidence of God's unwavering commitment to his people. The psalm ends by reminding them that all this was done so that they would keep his laws and follow his commandments.

### Psalm 117

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm117.jpg)

The shortest psalm in the entire collection— a two-verse call for all the goyim (foreign peoples) to praise God, grounded in a single reason: his kindness toward Israel has prevailed, and his faithfulness endures forever.

### Psalm 129

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm129.jpg)

A Pilgrim Song (Shir HaMa'alot, sung on the ascent to the Temple in Jerusalem), voiced as Israel looking back on a lifetime of persecution that never succeeded in destroying them. The image of plowers cutting long furrows across a back captures the depth of the suffering— yet God cut their cords. The psalm ends not with celebration but with a quiet, determined wish that all who hate Zion will wither like rooftop grass that no one bothers to harvest.

### Psalm 130

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm130.jpg)

A Pilgrim Song (Shir HaMa'alot, sung on the ascent to the Temple in Jerusalem), a cry from the absolute depths — not a request for help against enemies, but the soul's call to God in the face of its own wrongdoing. Yet the psalm does not remain in the abyss: forgiveness belongs to God, and the soul waits for Him more urgently than watchmen wait for dawn. The final verses open outward, calling all Israel to hope in a God who will redeem them from every sin.

### Psalm 137

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm137.jpg)

A psalm of exile, describing the grief of Israelites carried far from their homeland to Babylon. There, by the rivers, they wept as they remembered Zion, and hung up their harps because they could not bring themselves to sing the songs of their land for their captors. The psalm turns into a strong oath of loyalty to Jerusalem, and closes with a bitter cry for justice against those who destroyed the city.

### Psalm 150

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ReubenInstitute/Media/main/psalms/covers/square/psalm150.jpg)

The final psalm of the entire collection, a joyful call to praise God with every kind of music — wind instruments, strings, drums, and cymbals all join in. It then broadens to include everything that has breath, calling on all living things to praise God. It is a fitting, exuberant close to the Book of Psalms.
