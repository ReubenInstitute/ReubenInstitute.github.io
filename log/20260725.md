# 25 Jul 2026 – DeepSeek 3 Expert – Clock face visual design, halachic bands, night watches, moon arc, and the civil‑clock concept

_Code, HebrewClock_

With the JavaScript library stable and the live demo rendering correctly, the session turned to what the clock actually looks like and what information it conveys. By the end of the evening, the academic dial had acquired a full set of halachic prayer bands, a moon indicator, and a night‑watch background, and the concept for a civil‑clock version — the “killer feature” that would make the product accessible to a non‑specialist — had been fully articulated.

### Clock face polish

The standard clock received the first round of purely visual refinement. Major ticks at 12, 3, 6, and 9 were lengthened to 16 px via a CSS `:nth‑child(3n+1)` rule, making the cardinal hours immediately legible. The Hebrew clock’s tick layout was more demanding: major ticks were needed at sunset, midnight, sunrise, and noon, with three‑hour intermediates between them. A pure‑CSS approach failed because the DOM order and angular positions were offset; the fix was to adjust the JavaScript so that ticks were generated starting at the beginning of each hour, aligning sunset with child 1, after which a single `:nth‑child(3n+1)` rule could reach all the cardinal positions. The sunrise mark (child 13) was coloured black like the night ticks rather than the day‑tick grey.

Half‑hour marks were added inside both clock faces, shorter (10 px) than the hour marks, with grey for the day side and a lighter grey for the night side. They were drawn with a dedicated SVG string appended to the ticks container, keeping the mark generation logic inside the clock classes.

On the Hebrew clock, the minute hand was removed entirely — the seasonal hour is the natural unit of the dial, and a minute hand would have been misleading. The hour hand was lengthened to the old minute‑hand length (80 px) to give it visual weight against the expanding and contracting arcs. The second hand was made short (40 px total) and thin, set to complete one revolution per seasonal minute, producing a calm, continuous sweep that reinforces the different pace of the Hebrew day without distracting from the bands.

All clock drawing was moved from HTML‑and‑CSS to pure SVG, assembled dynamically in `Clock.js` from a dictionary of SVG strings. The standard clock kept all three hands and its minute ticks; only the Hebrew clock was simplified.

### Halachic bands

The most important visual addition to the academic dial was a ring of coloured arcs representing the daily zmanim. Each band is a fraction of the seasonal day, rendered as a stroked circular arc with stroke‑dasharray and stroke‑dashoffset, rotated so that the hour hand’s tip sweeps along them like a fuel gauge.

The morning bands form a progression of three deepening blues: a dark blue segment from misheyakir to sunrise (earliest tallit and tefillin), a brighter blue from sunrise to three seasonal hours (the Shema window), and a light blue for the remaining hour until the end of the Amidah window. After a transparent gap at midday, the afternoon bands pick up with a green segment from 6.5 to 9.5 seasonal hours (Mincha Gedolah), followed by two amber segments — a lighter one from 9.5 to 10.75 hours (Mincha Ketana before Pelag) and a darker one from 10.75 hours to sunset (Mincha Ketana after Pelag). The noon gap remained transparent, without bands, preserving the visual silence between the morning and afternoon prayer obligations. The night side was initially left transparent as well; that would be filled later.

### Night watches

The night arc (sunset to the next sunrise) was first drawn as a single silver‑grey pie segment, but almost immediately the decision was made to divide it into traditional ashmorot — three or four watches of the night, each rendered as a filled pie‑shaped wedge with a slightly different shade of grey. A setting was proposed that would let the user toggle between three and four watches, since both traditions appear in the sources (Berakhot 3a). The watch segments were kept completely separate from the halachic bands; the bands remain transparent over the night arc except for the small misheyakir‑to‑sunrise intrusion at dawn.

### Moon arc

A fourth ring, drawn at a smaller radius than the prayer bands, shows the period when the moon is above the horizon — a thin, pale amber arc from moonrise to moonset, computed from the `Moon` class and converted to seasonal hours. Because the moon’s visibility drifts by roughly 48 minutes each day, the arc shifts visibly against the fixed prayer bands, creating a lunisolar dashboard on the single clock face.

### Civil clock with moving bands

The academic dial is accurate and information‑dense, but it requires a user to learn an unfamiliar clock face — 24 unequal hours, a stationary night arc, and no 12‑at‑the‑top convention. The breakthrough of the session was the recognition that the same halachic band data could be overlaid onto a standard 12‑hour clock, and that a simple mechanical trick would make it work.

The idea: a conceptual 720° ring containing 24 civil hours’ worth of band data is mapped onto a 360° clock face, but only a 12‑hour forward‑looking window centred on the hour hand is made visible. A deliberate gap opposite the hour hand separates past from future, so the user sees a continuous ribbon of upcoming zmanim sweeping around the dial. As the hour hand advances, the window rotates, and the bands slide smoothly along with it. This converts the academic dial’s static arcs into a dynamic, glanceable display of “what’s next” — no learning curve, no specialised clock face.

This civil‑clock view was immediately designated as the product’s default mode. The academic dial would remain available as the advanced, scholarly view.

### Poster and marketing

The civil‑clock image, captured at a meaningful moment — just after sunset, with the evening bands sliding into view — was conceived as the poster for the product. The slogan: “The clock you didn’t know you needed,” and for the Haredi market, “יהודי יקר! אתה לא יודע עדיין שאתה צריך את זה.” The poster would contain no other words, no explanation, just the clock face itself. The Institute’s naming convention — “Hebrew clock,” “Hebrew holidays,” not “Jewish” — was reaffirmed as a deliberate choice that bypasses cultural labels and keeps the focus on the language and the mathematics.

### Halachic enums and Custom class

Beneath the visual design, the customisation system was formalised. The existing halachic opinion settings from the HebDate app were extracted into three C‑style enums in `Custom.js`, using descriptive prefixes to encode the measurement type:

- `DawnCustom` with seven values: `DEG_16_1`, `DEG_16_9`, `DEG_18`, `DEG_19_8`, `MIN_72`, `REL_MIN_72` (the duplicate `TENTH_OF_DAY` was removed).
- `MisheyakirCustom` with five values: `DEG_11_5`, `DEG_11`, `DEG_10_2`, `MIN_50`, `DEG_7_65`.
- `NightfallCustom` with twelve values, including `DEG_5_95`, `DEG_6`, `DEG_6_45`, `DEG_7_1`, `DEG_8`, `DEG_8_5`, `MIN_72`, `REL_MIN_72`, `DEG_16_1`, `REL_MIN_90` (for 1/8 of the day), `DEG_18`, `DEG_19_8`. The `TENTH_OF_DAY` and `EIGHTH_OF_DAY` entries were removed.

Each entry was documented with its common attribution — Gra, Magen Avraham, Rabbeinu Tam, and others — in a Markdown table that included angle‑to‑minute approximations. The `Custom` class was updated to hold a dawn, misheyakir, and nightfall value, and the preset table was wired into the clock’s band computation.

### Sun class refactoring

The `Sun` class, previously a set of static functions, was made mutable. The constructor now takes a location and a date, stores them, and calls an `update()` method that populates `riseTime`, `setTime`, and `noonTime`. New `setLocation` and `setDate` methods allow the object to be reused without re‑creation, and `dawnTime(angleDegrees)` and `duskTime(angleDegrees)` were added — parameterless aside from the depression angle — using the stored date and location. All astronomical helpers remained private; the public API became clean and stateful.

### Date and time span classes

The original `DaySpan` class was removed entirely. In its place, two clean span types were introduced: `DateSpan` for integer day offsets and `TimeSpan` for seconds. The `DateTime.add` method handles both: a `DateSpan` adjusts the date directly; a `TimeSpan` adds seconds and detects overflow by comparing the old and new time hours — if the result is smaller than the original when adding a positive span, the date advances. No `86400` constant appears anywhere in this logic except as the internal wrapping limit inside `Time`’s own constructor, where it represents the fixed definition of a civil‑day container, not a claim about the length of any astronomical or halachic day.

### HebrewDay and HebrewDayTime design

A new `HebrewDay` class was designed to take a civil `DateTime`, a `Location`, and a `Custom`, and pre‑compute all zmanim as public properties. The constructor determines the correct Hebrew date (sunset‑aware), finds the civil date containing that Hebrew day’s daylight, computes astronomical sunrise, sunset, and the next sunrise, derives day and night lengths, and then applies the dawn, nightfall, and misheyakir offsets. All derived zmanim — Shema, Amidah, Mincha Gedolah, and so on — are then computed as fractions of the halachic day length. The critical rule was that the dawn, nightfall, and misheyakir logic must use inline `if`/`else` chains on the enum constants directly, with no lookup tables and no helper methods. A first implementation was produced but rejected because it used lookup tables; the rewrite with inline checks remained pending.

`HebrewDayTime` was planned as a thin wrapper holding a `HebrewDay` and a seasonal‑hour float, with a `setDateTime` method to recompute the hour from the existing day’s boundaries without recalculating the zmanim unless the Hebrew date had actually changed.

### Special calendar events

Several date‑based events that are not holidays in the liturgical sense were added to `HebrewHoliday.js` as standalone functions: `omerCount`, `isNewMoonSanctificationDay`, `isSunBlessingDay`, `isTenDaysOfRepentancePeriod`, `isBeinHaMeitzarimPeriod`, and `isNineDaysOfAvPeriod`. The naming convention uses “Day” for single‑day events, “Period” for multi‑day ranges, and Hebrew terms where English is ambiguous. Each function receives a `HebrewDate` and requires no location.

The session left the clock with a fully specified visual language, a working halachic‑band ring, a moon indicator, night watches, a clear path to the civil‑clock interface, and a formalised customisation system. The remaining implementation work — finishing the `HebrewDay` inline checks, implementing `HebrewDayTime`, and building the civil‑clock band overlay — was deferred to the following sessions.
