# 8 Aug 2026 – DeepSeek 3 – Moon ring, observance ring, holiday enums, stripe drawing, and civil‑clock digits

_Code, HebrewClock_

The session began with the moon ring — a single band of amber that shows when the moon is above the horizon. By the end, the clock had four complete concentric rings, a fully corrected Hebrew holiday class with Erev days, an observance ring that marks fasts with diagonal stripes, and a civil clock that teaches 24‑hour time to anyone who can read a digit.

### Moon ring

The moon ring started as a simple idea: use the existing `Moon` class to get visibility intervals for the two civil days that overlap the yemama, clip to `yemamaStart`–`yemamaEnd`, and draw amber arcs. The colour came from a new `MoonPhaseType` enum — eight phases from `NEW_MOON` to `WANING_CRESCENT` — with a colour map that shifts from deep orange at new moon to bright amber at full. The `MoonIllumination` class was built to hold `fraction`, `phase` (0‑1 float), `angle` (bright‑limb angle), `phaseType`, and `phaseEmoji` — all computed properties, no stored state.

But the old `Moon` class was still date‑based with a stored midnight `_datetime`, and `Sun` was similarly anchored to a `Date`. Both were refactored to be **DateTime‑based**: the constructor takes a precise instant, and `celestialLocation` and `distance` are now properties that compute from that instant. The Moon gained a `Sun` instance, and `MoonIllumination` uses the two bodies’ directions and distances directly. `Moon.altitude` received a parallax correction — the Moon’s closeness to Earth means its topocentric altitude can differ by ~1° from the geocentric value, which visibly affects rise/set times. The rise/set scanning loop was extended to 25 points (hours 0‑24, with hour 24 being midnight of the next day) to catch crossings exactly at midnight.

### Holiday enums, Erev days, and the observance ring

A long, careful discussion clarified the distinction between a “holiday” (stores closed, work forbidden) and “Yom Tov” (the intermediate festival days where some work is permitted). The `HebrewHolidayId` enum was corrected: `PESACH` now correctly maps to 15 Nisan (not 14), `SUKKOT` to 15 Tishrei, and `HOSHANA_RABA` is now properly separated from `CHOL_HAMOED_SUKKOT`. Erev days — the civil day before a major closure day — were added as a separate boolean `erev` property on `HebrewHoliday`, keeping the enum clean and avoiding the Yom HaZikaron/Erev Yom HaAtzmaut conflict that had polluted earlier attempts.

The **observance ring** grew out of this. An `ObservanceBand` enum was created: `NONE`, `REST` (red), `REST_STRIPED` (red + diagonal stripes — Yom Kippur and Tisha B’Av), `YOMTOV` (pink — Chol HaMoed), `FAST` (yellow — minor fast day background), and `FAST_STRIPED` (yellow + stripes — actual fasting hours). The `LocalHebrewDate.observanceBands` property computes contiguous, non‑overlapping bands for the yemama, including the MA twilight “no‑man’s‑land”: when a rest day follows, the previous day’s bands are clipped to sunset, and the rest day’s band begins at sunset, not at nightfall. This makes visible the halachic ambiguity that the Magen Avraham system deliberately creates — the calendar date has already flipped, but the structured night watches haven’t started.

### Diagonal stripes

Drawing stripes on an annular sector turned into a multi‑hour battle. The first attempt used screen‑space 45° lines, which caused the stripes to spin as the band rotated with the civil clock’s hour hand. The second attempt rotated each stripe around the arc’s centre, which simply relocated the radial ticks to a new angle without tilting them. Claude eventually provided the correct fix: anchor the stripe grid to a fixed origin (`gridOrigin = -90°`), compute stripe positions by angle, build a short radial segment from `innerR` to `outerR` at that angle, then rotate that segment by 45° around **its own midpoint**. This creates short diagonal lines that follow the ring’s curvature and remain stationary on the clock face. The stripe colour was changed from black to white for visual contrast against the red and yellow backgrounds.

### Video generation for fast testing

A Python script using `ffmpeg‑python` was written to render the `StandardClock` every 10 minutes across each fast day — Tisha B’Av, Yom Kippur, 17 Tammuz, Tzom Gedaliah, 10 Tevet, and Ta’anit Esther. The start times initially had off‑by‑one errors (the 15:00 “before” snapshot was on the fast day itself for all fasts except 9 Av). Once corrected, the videos clearly show the observance ring appearing at sunset, the stripes covering the fast period, and everything dissolving back to normal at nightfall. Gemini analysed the silent videos and correctly identified the meaning of the red/yellow colours, the diagonal stripes, and the start/end at sunset/nightfall — validating that the visual language is intuitively understandable.

### Swapping 24‑hour digits

The civil clock now displays 24‑hour digits at the 12 major hour marks, sliding with the hour hand just like the bands. The digit opposite the hour hand is hidden in the seam gap. Each digit is rendered upright using per‑mark rotation logic, and the characters are drawn via a new `drawCurvedChar` method in `Display.py` that uses PIL to render a character at large scale, rotate it, downscale with Lanczos filtering, and blend it onto the pixel buffer. A flicker bug — the digit at the mark just clockwise from the hour hand would briefly show the previous hour — was traced to a floor‑division issue: `total_sec // 3600` floors a value slightly below an exact hour. The fix was `int(round(total_sec / 3600.0)) % 24`, which rounds to the nearest hour.

### The session’s residue

The four rings are now complete and correct: Sun (day/night), Observance (rest days, fasts), Halakhic (zmanim + three night watches), and Moon (visibility with phase colour). The `HebrewHoliday` logic is clean, with Erev days as a separate property. The stripe‑drawing code is stable and anchored to the ring, not the band. The digits teach 24‑hour time silently. The fast‑transition videos are ready for social media. What remains are text labels — Hebrew date, Gregorian date, holiday name, period name, parashah — and a handful of minor zmanim like candle‑lighting and chametz time.
