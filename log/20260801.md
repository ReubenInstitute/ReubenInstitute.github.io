# 1 Aug 2026 – DeepSeek 3 Expert – Manual image generation, SVG script, and the decision to port the core classes to Python

_Documentation, HebrewClock_

The manual for the Hebrew Clock needed images — not approximate illustrations, but precise dial renderings that matched what the device would actually display for a given date, location, and custom. The session produced a Python SVG generator, a full set of academic dial images, and the realisation that every angle in the manual must come from the same code that runs on the hardware.

### Academic view specification

The manual’s academic dial was formalised with a fixed orientation: midnight at the bottom, midday at the top, sunrise on the left, sunset on the right, and the night arc filling the lower portion of the face. Marks were standardised: four major marks at sunset, midnight, sunrise, and midday, eight smaller hour marks, and twelve half‑hour marks.

The first two images generated — `academic-dial-summer.svg` and `academic-dial-winter.svg` — showed the hour and second hands positioned at the end of the Shema period (the third seasonal hour). The captions highlighted the different clock angles of the same halachic moment in summer versus winter, making the seasonal variation immediately visible. Hour labels were placed outside the dial: day hours labelled with Hebrew letters א through יב, rotating with the angle of the hour mark so they read radially outward. Night watch labels א through ד were rotated an additional 180° so they appeared upright when the watch segment was at the bottom of the dial.

### Night watch images

Two standalone images were produced showing only the night‑watch wedges, without the full dial or marks. The four‑watch image, `night-watches-4.svg`, divided the night into four equal grey segments using the four night‑shade colours defined in the specification. The three‑watch image, `night-watches-3.svg`, split the middle watch at midnight to keep the cardinal point visible, producing four unequal segments despite representing only three watches. Watch labels א–ג or א–ד were placed outside the wedges.

### Halakhic ring

The full halachic band ring was rendered in both summer and winter configurations. All eight prayer bands were drawn — Dawn, Misheyakir, Shema, Amidah, Mincha Gedolah, Mincha Ketana, Plag Mincha, and Nightfall — as annular ring segments rather than stroked arcs, avoiding the stroke‑dasharray artefacts that had caused rendering problems earlier.

The initial Dawn and Nightfall bands were too narrow because the depression angles had not been correctly converted to dial angles. The fix used the actual night length to compute the angular span of the 16.1° dawn depression and the 11.5° nightfall depression, widening the bands to their correct size. The colour scheme was adjusted: Dawn took the old Misheyakir colour, Misheyakir took the old Shema colour, Shema took the old Amidah colour, and Amidah received a new light blue (`#b0c4de`). Hour and second hands and labels were included on these images.

### Moon ring

A separate image, `academic-moon-ring.svg`, isolated the moon arc. A realistic summer day was chosen — 20 Jun 2026, Jerusalem — giving a moonrise around 21:30 and moonset around 09:45. The dial angles were calculated from the civil times and the seasonal hour mapping, producing an amber arc (`#d4a017`) spanning 131.78° to 315.69°. A moon‑phase emoji was placed at the midnight position, and a legend of moon‑phase emojis with four muted‑orange shades was added, running from darkest at new moon to brightest at full.

### The Python script

The entire image set was generated by a single Python script, `images.py`, using only `math`, `jinja2`, and `os`. All dimensions, colours, and mark lengths were stored as module‑level constants. A set of SVG helper functions — `_svg_line`, `_svg_pie`, `_svg_hand`, `_svg_ring_segment`, `_svg_label` — assembled the elements, and two Jinja2 templates (`SVG_TEMPLATE` for the full dial and `NIGHT_WATCH_TEMPLATE` for the wedges) provided the document structure. Output functions wrote `.svg` files and a concatenated `svgs.md` reference file.

### The hardcoded‑angle problem

Despite the visual accuracy of the output, the script was built on fake data. Summer and winter daylight lengths were hardcoded constants. The dawn and nightfall angles were baked‑in minutes. The moon arc used a single fixed date. The images looked correct, but they were not driven by the actual zmanim of any real date, location, or custom.

This was recognised as a fundamental problem: the manual must show exactly what the device displays, and the device’s clock face is the output of a halachic‑math pipeline. Any discrepancy between the manual’s images and the real clock face — even a single pixel of band width — would be a published inaccuracy.

### The decision to port

The fix was not to improve the hardcoded script but to replace it entirely. The core calendar and astronomy classes — `Date`, `Time`, `DateTime`, `HebrewDate`, `Sun`, `Moon`, `Custom`, `LocalHebrewDate` — would be ported from C++ to Python in a future session. The image generator would then import these classes, instantiate them for specific dates, locations, and customs, and draw the dial directly from the zmanim they produced. Every band, every hand, and every watch segment would be a true rendering of the device’s own computation.

Until that port was complete, the existing images would serve as placeholders in the manual, clearly marked as provisional. The manual’s Display section was marked “fake — pending Python port.”

The session ended with a complete set of manual images, a reusable SVG generation script, and an unambiguous path forward: the manual would be true to the device, or it would not be published.
