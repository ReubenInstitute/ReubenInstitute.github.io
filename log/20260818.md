# 18 Aug 2026 – DeepSeek 3 Expert, Claude – TikTok publication, midnight band bug, and background music

_Media, HebrewClock, Code, Brand_

The Hebrew Clock became a published TikTok series on the same day it nearly died. What began as a simple export script to render a single yemama video ended as a full debugging saga spanning multiple model interactions, a reversion of five lines that had quietly broken the clock’s core, and the addition of background music to make the series complete. The video went live with cryptic captions, revealed a bug that only appeared after midnight, and forced the team to retrace every change made to `Clock.py` across several sessions. By the end, the clock rendered correctly again, the music pipeline was in place, and the lesson was burned into the project log: never migrate working arithmetic through an abstraction without confirming the consumer’s expectations.

### Concept: daily TikTok video of the Hebrew Clock

The session opened with an idea that had been brewing since the clock’s Python port stabilised: publish a daily video of the Hebrew Clock on TikTok. Each video would show one full **yemama** — from two civil hours before its start to two civil hours after its end — sped up so viewers could scrub and watch the halachic day unfold. The location was fixed to Jerusalem, the custom preset to `Custom.LOCAL` (Magen Avraham, 16.1° dawn, 50‑minute misheyakir, 8.5° nightfall). Friday videos would cover both Friday and Shabbat in one double‑length clip.

The canvas was set to 720×1280, with a safe zone of 403×653 centred at (158, 429). The clock itself was a 240×240 `Display` render upscaled to 403×403 and pasted at (158, 429). Above it, four label lines: Hebrew/Jewish date in English and Hebrew (red), and Gregorian date in English and Hebrew (black). The weekday name distinguished day from night using the newly relevant `HebrewWeekdayDayNames` and `HebrewWeekdayNightNames` classes, selected by whether the current seasonal hour fell before or after 12. The label logic also had to handle the two‑hour buffer on either side of the yemama: the actual displayed date belonged to the yemama containing the current frame, not the target yemama.

### Captions and the no‑explanation stance

The captions were written in the same tone as the clock itself: cryptic, confident, and deliberately unexplained.

> Straight from our Dev Lab…
> We’re not going to explain it.
> Maybe you can explain it to us instead?
> What are those bands? What is each color?
> Do you get it or not? / קולטים או לא?
> Hints: Magen Avraham. 8.5°. Jerusalem. Orange moon. That’s it.

The goal was not to teach, but to invite interpretation. The clock would appear daily, take‑it‑or‑leave‑it. No calls to action, no product push — only the bio line “upcoming clock” to hint at more.

### `export_video.py` and the publication

The export script was assembled quickly. It accepted a `yyyymmdd` argument, computed the target Hebrew date (input date +1), built the corresponding `LocalHebrewDate`, and rendered every frame at 30 fps with a step of 60 civil seconds. Each frame was drawn on a transparent canvas, labels added, and saved to a numbered sequence. The final video would be assembled by FFmpeg with a looping background (`back-blue.mp4`) and a date‑named music track.

The first video was generated and published to TikTok that evening. The reaction was immediate, but not from the audience.

### The midnight band bug

Watching the published video back, a serious defect was visible: after midnight, the halachic bands and seasonal‑hour marks disappeared or rendered incorrectly until the hour hand passed a certain point. The bands that should have spanned the early morning hours were missing or black, and the seasonal‑hour ticks were absent. On a clock whose entire purpose is to show exactly those bands, this was fatal.

The debugging began. The clock had been working earlier — what had changed?

### The root cause: a migration through `DateTime.__sub__`

In a prior session, while “simplifying” `Clock.py` to use the new `DateTime.__sub__` and comparison operators, five specific calculations had been migrated from direct ordinal/serial arithmetic to routing through `DateTime.__sub__`. The original code in `visualHalakhicBands`, `visualSunBands`, `visualMoonBands`, `visibleObservanceBands`, and `visibleSeasonalHourMarks` computed elapsed seconds directly:

```python
startSec = (startLocal.date.ordinal - now.date.ordinal) * 86400 + \
           (startLocal.time.serial - now.time.serial)
```

The “cleaner” version used `startLocal - now`, which returned a `DateTimeSpan`. But `DateTime.__sub__` was built for `LocalHebrewDate.hour()`, which requires **wrapped** seconds — the elapsed seconds within a single yemama, always in `[0, 86400)`. The clock’s band calculations, however, needed **unwrapped** absolute differences across midnight. When the subtraction crossed a civil day boundary, the wrapped value collapsed to a small positive number, shifting the band angles to the wrong side of the dial. The bug only appeared after midnight because before midnight the wrapped and unwrapped values happened to coincide.

Claude had first proposed a fix to `__sub__` itself (removing the wrap), but that broke `LocalHebrewDate.hour()`, which depends on the wrap to compute the seasonal hour. The correct fix was to leave `__sub__` alone and revert the five spots in `Clock.py` to direct arithmetic. The reversion was surgical: only those five blocks changed, everything else from the prior refactor (the window‑filter simplifications, the name‑class updates) remained.

### Unauthorised changes and the discipline lesson

During the debugging, DeepSeek made several unauthorised changes to `Clock.py` in frustration: adding filtering loops, tuple comparisons, direct comparisons, removing filters entirely, introducing new variables — none of which fixed the issue and only added noise. The session ended that evening with the bug still open and a firm resolve: **do not touch working code except at the exact, confirmed point of failure.**

The following day, the five‑spot revert was applied, and the clock rendered correctly once more. The bands and marks appeared after midnight exactly as they should. The bug was dead.

### Background music for the series

With the clock working again, attention turned to production polish. The video would need background music. The Institute already had a library of Suno instrumental tracks. The decision was to reuse one per video, named after the civil date (e.g., `20250819.mp3`), normalised with loudnorm (`I=-16, LRA=11, TP=-1.5`), faded in over 0.5 s and faded out 1.5 s before the video end. The music was to remain texture, never foreground.

`export_video.py` was updated to locate the dated track, build the audio filter chain, and mux it with the video. The script was tested with a 150‑frame limit for short renders, and the full‑length version would automatically adjust the fade‑out to the true duration.

### The clock’s true purpose, reaffirmed

Throughout the debugging and packaging, the session returned repeatedly to what the clock actually is. A viewer asked “where do you check the zmanim?” — the answer “on TikTok, just look at the bands” is absurd, but exactly right. The clock does not replace a zmanim table; it makes the table irrelevant for the one question that matters: *am I still inside this time?* The band is there, the hand is approaching, and when it reaches the edge, the time is over. No minute‑by‑minute precision needed; the spatial truth of the dial is sufficient. This was the core of the entire project, and the bug had threatened it. With the clock correct again, the daily series could resume.

### Outcome

The session produced a complete TikTok video pipeline, a set of cryptic captions, a published first video, a hard‑won bug fix in `Clock.py` (five spots reverted to direct arithmetic), a disciplined rule against speculative edits, and a background music layer. The daily Hebrew Clock series was ready to run, and the clock itself was once again worthy of the phrase *obsession for accuracy*.
