# 3 Aug 2026 – Claude Sonnet 5 – Second‑hand bug, timezone seam, and the civil clock’s cream ring

_Code, HebrewClock_

The session opened on a wounded codebase. The Python clock classes were ported, the display engine was drawing pixels, but the Hebrew clock’s second hand was still frozen to the hour hand and the civil clock was quietly lying to the user — Mincha Gedolah appearing at 10 am, nightfall at 5 pm, and a stray blue band bleeding through the seam opposite the hour hand. Four bugs stood between the Institute and a publishable clock face. By evening, all four were dead, and only a single file had been touched.

### Second‑hand bug: seasonal minute sweep

The Hebrew clock’s second hand was mathematically identical to the hour hand’s own position. Both were derived from the same continuous seasonal‑hour float. The first fix attempted was to make the second hand sweep 360° once per seasonal hour by using only the fractional part of the hour, multiplied by 360. That gave a sweeping second hand, but it was visually ambiguous: a “minute” on a seasonal clock is meaningless without saying whether it’s a day‑minute or a night‑minute, since they have different real‑time lengths.

The final fix used `hourFrac * 60 % 1.0` to isolate progress through the current seasonal *minute*, then multiplied by 360 to sweep the hand once per seasonal minute. Because `hourFrac` already carried the correct day/night arc share, the second hand automatically sped up in the shorter winter day minutes and slowed down in the longer summer night minutes — no explicit day/night branch required. The hour hand remained untouched. The two hands separated, each moving at its own rhythm.

### Draw‑order fix

A smaller visual bug: tick marks were rendering on top of the coloured bands, leaving grey notches cut into the halachic rings. The draw order was re‑sequenced in both `draw_standard_clock` and `draw_hebrew_clock`: bands first, then tick marks, then hands, cap, and rim. The rings became clean, unbroken arcs.

### The timezone seam: Mincha at 10 am

The civil clock’s bands were positioned disastrously wrong. Mincha Gedolah appeared mid‑morning, nightfall showed mid‑afternoon, and a pale blue band — Amidah — sat just after nightfall. The diagnosis was immediate once the right question was asked: `Sun.py` and `LocalHebrewDate.py` compute all zmanim in UTC. The civil clock was comparing those UTC times directly against a local IDT `now`, a mismatch of three hours in Jerusalem summer. Combined with the Magen Avraham day definition, the bands collapsed into the wrong part of the civil day.

The first attempt to fix it was a mistake. The +3 hour offset was inserted into `Sun.py`’s Julian‑day conversions, pushing the shift into the astronomical calculation layer. The user rejected this forcefully: `Sun.py` must remain pure UTC; other code may depend on it. The fix was reverted.

The correct fix was confined to a single class: `StandardClock` in `Clock.py`. A private constant `_STANDARD_CLOCK_DISPLAY_OFFSET_SECONDS = 3 * 3600` was added, and applied only within `StandardClock.bands` and `StandardClock.visualBands`. The offset shifts the UTC zman times to IDT purely for that class’s comparisons and plotting. `HebrewClock` needed no change — its seasonal‑hour math subtracts `hour()` from `yemamaStart`, and a constant offset cancels out of a subtraction. Every other file — `Sun.py`, `Date.py`, `LocalHebrewDate.py`, `HebrewDate.py`, `Custom.py`, `Geo.py` — was left untouched. The bands snapped to their correct positions. Mincha Gedolah was Mincha Gedolah again.

### Blue band after nightfall: the seam bug

Even after the timezone fix, a stray blue band — Amidah — persisted just after nightfall. The root cause was in how `StandardClock`’s visible window was mapped onto the dial face. The band window spans ±12 hours, so computed angles can range up to ±360°. The draw loop applied a modulo 360, folding the entire ±360° range onto a single 360° face. An event roughly ten hours away in the past could land on top of an unrelated event two hours in the future, producing an impossible ghost band.

The fix was to clip each band to a visible half‑window (±180° = ±6 hours) before mapping to the face, and to cut a small gap out of the antipodal point — the point opposite the hour hand — so that past and future content never touched there. The modulo was removed. The blue ghost vanished.

### Seam gap widening

The initial gap was 4°, barely visible. The user asked for a gap the width of five minutes on a standard clock face: 30°. The constant `SEAM_GAP_DEGREES` was set to 30.0. The antipodal gap became a clear, deliberate break in the ring.

### Cream base ring

One final visual refinement: the “silent” stretches between zmanim — the noon gap between Amidah and Mincha Gedolah, for instance — were showing as plain page background, visually indistinguishable from the seam gap. The user wanted only the one true seam to read as a gap. A cream base ring (`RING_TRACK_COLOR = 0xE8DFC8`, a shade slightly off the `0xFAF0E6` page background) was drawn first across the entire visible window — from `lo` to `hi`, excluding the seam — and then the individual coloured zman bands were drawn on top. Any stretch of time with no active zman now showed as a continuous cream ring with nothing happening; only the deliberate seam cut showed bare page.

### Result

At the end of the session, `Clock.py` was the sole modified file, verified end‑to‑end by running the `__main__` demo and inspecting the rendered PNGs at each fix. `Sun.py` remained pure UTC. The +3 hour IDT shift was a documented, display‑only, single‑file workaround pending real timezone support. The Hebrew clock ticked correctly. The civil clock told halachic truth.

The Python pipeline was ready for the manual — or for a product shot.
