# Lag BaOmer

## Introduction

## Part 1: Foundations

The story of Lag BaOmer begins not with a holiday, but with a gap. Between the liberation of Pesach and the revelation of Shavuot lie forty‑nine days—a period known as the *Omer*, named after the ancient barley offering brought to the Temple. For centuries, these weeks carried a quiet tension. By the time the *Shulchan Aruch* codified Jewish law in the sixteenth century, the Omer had become a season of semi‑mourning—a collective pause in Jewish joy. Yet, one day stood out as a rupture in that solemnity:

> *“Some have the custom to rejoice on the thirty‑third day of the Omer.”* —Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 493:2

What made the thirty‑third day—*Lag* (ל״ג) in Hebrew numerals—different? The answer lies in the layers of Talmud, history, and heartbreak that shaped the day’s identity.

### The Plague of Rabbi Akiva’s Students

The earliest anchor for the Omer’s mournful character appears in the Babylonian Talmud. It records a staggering intellectual and spiritual catastrophe: the death of an entire generation of scholars.

> *“It was taught: Rabbi Akiva had twelve thousand pairs of disciples … all of them died between Pesach and Shavuot because they did not treat each other with respect.”* —Talmud Bavli, Yevamot 62b

The tragedy was not merely the loss of life, but the reason behind it. The sages noted that these students, despite their brilliance, failed in the most basic human requirement: empathy. The Tosefta expands on this, noting they looked upon one another with a "harsh eye." The result was a "bad death"—an epidemic of croup that left the world of Torah desolate:

> *“And the world was desolate of Torah until Rabbi Akiva went to the south and taught his later disciples: Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Yehuda, Rabbi Yose, Rabbi Shimon [bar Yochai], and Rabbi Elazar ben Shammua.”* —Jerusalem Talmud, Sanhedrin 10:2

Tradition holds that on the thirty‑third day of the Omer, the dying finally stopped. This single day became a "breathing room" in the mourning—a moment when the shadow of loss gave way to the possibility of a new beginning.

### The Bar Kochba Revolt

While the Talmud speaks of a plague among scholars, history records a simultaneous physical struggle. In the second century, the Jewish people launched a desperate bid for independence against the Roman Empire: the Bar Kochba Revolt (132–136 CE). 

It was a total war. The Roman historian Cassius Dio described a Judea turned to a wasteland, with hundreds of villages destroyed. The spiritual leader of this revolt was none other than Rabbi Akiva himself, who saw in the rebel leader, Shimon ben Kosiba (Bar Kochba), a potential Messiah. The fall of the final stronghold, Beitar, was so devastating that the rabbis later compared its destruction to the fall of the Temple:

> *“The slaughter at Beitar was immense … the blood of the slain flowed into the sea.”* —Talmud Bavli, Gittin 57a

Among the ruins of this failed revolution, a tradition persisted that the thirty‑third day of the Omer marked a rare moment of military success or a miraculous respite from Roman slaughter. Whether it was a victory on the battlefield or the end of a literal plague, Lag BaOmer became the day the tide briefly turned in favor of the oppressed.

### Early Observances

For centuries following the Talmudic era, the Omer remained a time of strict restraint: no weddings, no haircuts, no public celebrations. Curiously, the earliest prayer books, such as the *Siddur* of Saadia Gaon, make no mention of a holiday on the thirty-third day. 

It wasn't until the Geonic period (roughly 6th–11th centuries) that the specific tradition of the plague's end began to surface in legal responsa. By the 13th century, the custom of rejoicing had crossed into Europe. Rabbi Abraham ben Nathan ha‑Yarḥi recorded it in his *Sefer HaManhig*, and Rabbi Eleazar of Worms noted it among the communities of Ashkenaz. 

Later commentators, like the *Magen Avraham*, explicitly linked the joy to Bar Kochba’s temporary triumph:

> *“Some explain that the thirty‑third day is a day of joy because it was a victory day for Bar Kochba.”* —Magen Avraham on Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 493:2

By the time the *Shulchan Aruch* was printed in the 1500s, Lag BaOmer had secured its place. It stood on a composite foundation of memory: the survival of Rabbi Akiva’s final five students, the grit of the Bar Kochba rebels, and the stubborn Jewish hope that even in the deepest mourning, the darkness eventually lifts. What began as a quiet gap in a mournful count was becoming a festival of resilience.



## Part 2: Evolution

For centuries after the Talmud, the Omer remained a season of restraint. Weddings were postponed, hair was left uncut, and public celebrations were avoided. The thirty‑third day, if it was marked at all, was little more than a whispered tradition—a day when the plague among Rabbi Akiva’s students had supposedly ceased. But by the early medieval period, Lag BaOmer began to emerge from the shadows, appearing in the works of the *Rishonim*, the great scholars of Europe.

In the early thirteenth century, Rabbi Abraham ben Nathan ha‑Yarḥi, in his *Sefer HaManhig*, recorded a practice that was still taking shape:

> *“It is customary to rejoice on the thirty‑third day of the Omer, because on that day the students of Rabbi Akiva stopped dying.”* —Sefer HaManhig, early 13th c.

Around the same time, Rabbi Eleazar of Worms noted that the custom had crossed into Ashkenaz. Yet, even then, the nature of the “rejoicing” was modest—a brief pause in mourning rather than a full‑blown festival.

### The Zohar and  Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai

The transformation of Lag BaOmer into a major celebration came from an unexpected source: the **Zohar**, the foundational text of Kabbalah. The Zohar centers on Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai (Rashbi), a second‑century sage and disciple of Rabbi Akiva. Tradition tells of him hiding in a cave for thirteen years to escape Roman decree, emerging as the master of the "hidden" Torah.

The Zohar describes the final day of his life—not as a tragedy, but as a moment of unparalleled revelation. In the *Idra Zuta* (“The Small Assembly”), it narrates his passing:

> *“While Rabbi Shimon was speaking, fire surrounded the house … and he said: ‘This is the day of my joy, the day of my wedding, the day of my ascent.’”* —Zohar, Idra Zuta III, 287b–296b

The Zohar calls the day of a righteous master's death a **Hilula**—an Aramaic term meaning “wedding.” In the mystical worldview, death is the ultimate union between the soul and its Divine source. By the sixteenth century, the identification was complete: Lag BaOmer was fixed as the anniversary of Rashbi’s passing. The mournful Omer gave way to a burst of fire, song, and mystical joy.

### The Safed Revolution

The decisive moment in the evolution of Lag BaOmer came in the Galilean city of **Safed** in the 1500s. There, a circle of kabbalists led by Rabbi Isaac Luria (the Ari) transformed the day into a structured ritual. The Ari’s disciples recorded that on this day, the soul of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai reaches its highest spiritual peak.

The Ari established the custom of the **Pilgrimage to Meron**, the village where Rashbi is buried. The kabbalists began lighting bonfires to symbolize the "pillar of fire" that reportedly surrounded Rashbi’s house at his death. This practice spread rapidly from the Ottoman Empire to Europe, turning a local Galilean custom into a global phenomenon.

### Codification

While the mystics were elevating the day, the legal authorities were formally protecting its status. Rabbi Yosef Karo recorded the basic joy in the *Shulchan Aruch*, but it was the gloss of **Rabbi Moshe Isserles (the Rema)** that finalized the permission for Ashkenazi Jews to break the mourning:

> *“On the thirty‑third day of the Omer, the mourning is lifted, and it is permitted to marry and to cut hair.”* —Rema, gloss to Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 493:2

By the 17th century, popular customs began to fuse the different traditions. The *Mateh Moshe* provides a rare glimpse into the folk traditions of Eastern Europe:

> *“It is the custom to make bonfires on Lag BaOmer, and children go out with bows and arrows.”* —Mateh Moshe, 16th c.

The bows and arrows likely served as a double-symbol: a nod to the military grit of the **Bar Kochba** rebels and a mystical reference to the "rainbow" (also *keshet* in Hebrew) which, according to the Talmud, never appeared during Rashbi's lifetime because his merit alone protected the world.

### The Hasidic Embrace

As Hasidism rose in the 18th century, Lag BaOmer took on personal, psychological dimensions. The Hasidic masters saw in Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai the archetype of the "Hidden Tzaddik" whose light can break through even the darkest soul. 

Rabbi Nachman of Breslov taught that the light revealed on this day has the power to "awaken" those who are spiritually asleep. Hasidic dynasties adopted the **Tish** (festive table), often held around a massive bonfire, turning the night into a showcase of ecstatic song and dance that continues until dawn.

### A Festival of Survival

By the end of the nineteenth century, Lag BaOmer had completed its journey. It had absorbed the memory of the plague's end, the defiance of Bar Kochba, and the mystical fire of the Zohar. The *Kitzur Shulchan Aruch* could finally describe a holiday with a fixed, recognizable shape: a day of bonfires, bows, and the lifting of mourning. 

What began as a quiet gap in the count of the Omer had become a festival of light—a celebration of the "hidden" Torah and the enduring Jewish refusal to let tragedy have the final word.
