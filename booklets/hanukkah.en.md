# Hanukkah: The Fire and the Light

## Introduction

*"A little bit of light pushes away a lot of darkness."*

Hanukkah, the Festival of Lights, occupies a unique place in the Jewish consciousness. Unlike the biblical festivals instituted by Moses in the desert, or Purim which was established by Prophets in Persia, Hanukkah is a Rabbinic holiday born from a clash of civilizations on the soil of Israel. It commemorates the victory of the weak over the strong, the few over the many, and spiritual purity over material might.

Falling during the darkest time of the year, when the nights are longest, Hanukkah is not merely a memorial of an ancient battle. It is an active protest against despair. This booklet traces the holiday's journey from the guerrilla battlefields of the Judean hills to the mystical texts of Safed, exploring how a celebration of military victory evolved into a cosmic meditation on the hidden light of creation.

## Part 1: Foundations

*"From the battlefield to the Beit Midrash."*

The origins of Hanukkah are dual in nature: they begin with a gritty historical war recorded in the books of the Maccabees and culminate in a spiritual miracle immortalized in the Talmud. To understand Hanukkah, one must look at both the "fire" of the revolt and the "light" of the Menorah.

### The Prophetic Prequel
Long before the Maccabees raised their swords, the prophets of Israel laid the spiritual groundwork for the holiday. The Prophet Haggai, encouraging the people to rebuild the Second Temple after the Babylonian exile, delivered a specific message regarding the **24th of Kislev**:

> *"Consider, I pray you, from this day and forward, from the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month [Kislev], even from the day that the foundation of the Lord's temple was laid..."* —Haggai 2:18

Centuries later, the Maccabean revolt would culminate in the rededication of this very Temple on the **25th of Kislev**, fulfilling the prophetic significance of the date. Similarly, the Prophet Zechariah shared a vision that would become the Hanukkah Haftarah, emphasizing that true victory comes not from armies, but from the Divine Spirit:

> *"I see a lampstand [Menorah] all of gold... and two olive trees by it... 'Not by might, nor by power, but by My spirit,' says the Lord of Hosts."* —Zechariah 4:2-6

### The Historical Victory: Sefer Josippon
While the Books of Maccabees recount the revolt, Jewish tradition largely preserved this history through **Sefer Josippon**, a 10th-century Hebrew adaptation of Josephus’s writings that became the accepted historical record for medieval Jewry.

The conflict began when the Seleucid King Antiochus IV Epiphanes sought to force Hellenism upon the Jews. This was not just a military occupation but a cultural war. He outlawed the three pillars of Jewish identity: **Shabbat**, **Circumcision** (Brit Milah), and **Rosh Chodesh** (The New Moon). He transformed the Holy Temple into a pagan shrine, erecting a statue of Zeus on the altar and sacrificing swine. In the towns, gymnasiums were built where Jewish youth were encouraged to hide the mark of their circumcision and embrace Greek philosophy and aesthetics.

In the town of Modi'in, the resistance ignited. The elderly priest Mattathias and his five sons—the Maccabees—refused to sacrifice to idols. Launching a guerrilla war from the caves and hills of Judea, they used their knowledge of the terrain to ambush the superior, elephant-mounted Greek armies.

Against all odds, the Jewish forces, led by Judah Maccabee, recaptured Jerusalem in 164 BCE. They found the Temple in ruins, the altar desecrated, and the golden vessels stolen. On the **25th of Kislev**, they rededicated the altar (*Chanukat HaMizbeach*). According to historical sources, this initial celebration was modeled after **Sukkot**—an eight-day festival the fighters had been unable to observe while hiding in the caves. They waved palm branches and lulavim, singing hymns of praise for eight days.

### The Rabbinic Establishment: The Pivot to Oil
While the war secured Jewish sovereignty, the Sages of the Talmud shifted the focus from military might to Divine intervention. In the famous passage in Tractate Shabbat, the Rabbis ask a fundamental question:

> *"Mai Chanukah? (What is Hanukkah?)"* —Talmud Bavli, Shabbat 21b

The Talmud's answer ignores the battles entirely and focuses on a single spiritual event. It relates that when the Greeks entered the Sanctuary, they systematically defiled all the oils used for the Menorah. When the Hasmoneans finally liberated the Temple, they searched the ruins and found only one small cruse of oil that still bore the unbroken seal of the High Priest.

> *"And there was sufficient oil there to light [the candelabrum] for only one day. A miracle occurred and they lit from it for eight days. The next year, the Sages instituted those days and made them holidays with recitation of Hallel and thanksgiving."* —Talmud Bavli, Shabbat 21b

This Talmudic "pivot" demilitarized the holiday. By anchoring the celebration in the miracle of the lights rather than the defeat of the Greeks, the Sages ensured Hanukkah’s survival even after Jewish sovereignty was lost to the Romans. It transformed Hanukkah from a celebration of independence into a testament to the endurance of the Jewish spirit.

## Part 2: Evolution

*"How did the law keep up with the exile?"*

As the Jewish people moved from the sovereignty of the Hasmoneans into the long night of the Diaspora, the observance of Hanukkah evolved. The theoretical laws of the Talmud had to adapt to the dangerous realities of life in Europe and the Middle East, leading to significant changes in how the holiday is practiced today.

### From Outdoors to Indoors
The Talmud originally mandated that the Hanukkah lamp be placed *"at the entrance of one's house on the outside,"* facing the street. The goal was **Pirsumei Nisa**—publicizing the miracle to every passerby.

However, as anti-Semitism rose in Babylonia (where Zoroastrians forbade fire worship) and later in Christian Europe, placing a burning sign of Jewish identity in the street became physically dangerous. The Sages enacted a crucial leniency:
> *"And in a time of danger, one places it on the table and that is sufficient."* —Talmud Bavli, Shabbat 21b

This ruling, codified in the *Shulchan Aruch*, moved the Menorah from the public courtyard to the private dining table or window. This shifted the "publicity" of the miracle from the world at large to the members of one's own household, transforming Hanukkah into a domestic, intimate family celebration.

### The "Ashkenazi" Upgrade
The Talmud describes three levels of observing the mitzvah:
1.  **Basic:** One lamp per household, per night.
2.  **Beautiful (*Mehadrin*):** One lamp per person in the house, per night.
3.  **Most Beautiful (*Mehadrin min HaMehadrin*):** Adding a candle each night to correspond to the days.

A major divergence occurred in how to interpret the highest level. **Sephardic** tradition, following Rabbi Yosef Karo, maintains that the head of the household lights *one* Menorah for the entire family, and that single Menorah gains a candle each night. **Ashkenazic** tradition, following the Rema (Rabbi Moses Isserles), adopted a maximalist custom where *every* member of the family lights their own Menorah. This evolution created the spectacular visual of modern Ashkenazi households, where a family of five might have five separate Menorahs burning simultaneously, creating a blazing "forest" of lights on the windowsill.

### The Rise of Dairy and Judith
While the Talmud focuses on oil, a medieval custom emerged to eat dairy foods on Hanukkah. This practice is linked to the story of **Judith**, a heroic widow who lived during the era of the Assyrian siege (often conflated with the Maccabean period in Jewish folklore).

Tradition recounts that Judith infiltrated the enemy camp of the general Holofernes. She fed him salty cheese to make him thirsty, forcing him to drink copious amounts of strong wine. When he fell into a drunken stupor, she beheaded him with his own sword, causing the enemy army to flee.
> *"Therefore, there is a custom to eat cheese on Hanukkah."* —Ran on Shabbat 10a

This custom evolved into a rich culinary tradition, introducing cheese blintzes, rugelach with cream cheese, and savory cheese pancakes to the Hanukkah table alongside the fried foods.

### The Forgotten Scroll: Megillat Antiochus
Just as Purim has *Megillat Esther*, Hanukkah once had its own scroll. **Megillat Antiochus**, an Aramaic account of the Maccabean revolt, was widely read in synagogues during the Geonic period. It opens with the grandeur of the Greek empire and details the battles and the miracle.

While this custom faded in most Ashkenazi communities, it was famously championed by **Saadia Gaon** and preserved in the liturgies of **Yemenite** and **Italian** Jews. In these communities, the scroll was read publicly on the Shabbat of Hanukkah for centuries, preserving a unique liturgical link to the history of the revolt.

## Part 3: Practice

*"So, how do we actually do this?"*

The observance of Hanukkah centers on the home and the synagogue, blending the physical act of lighting with specific liturgical additions and festive customs.

### Lighting the Menorah
The central mitzvah is *Hadlakat Nerot Chanukah*.
* **The Shamash:** A "servant" candle is used to kindle the others. It must be placed apart from the holy lights (usually higher or lower) to ensure we do not derive practical benefit (like reading) from the mitzvah candles.
* **Placement:** The candles are placed in the Menorah from **Right to Left** (the same direction as Hebrew script).
* **Lighting:** We light the candles from **Left to Right**. This signifies that we always start with the *new* candle of the day, honoring the increase in the miracle.
 
* **The Blessings:**
    * On the first night, three blessings are recited: 1) *Lehadlik Ner* (to light the candle), 2) *Sheasah Nissim* (Who performed miracles), and 3) *Shehecheyanu* (Who has kept us alive to this season).
    * On all subsequent nights, only the first two blessings are recited.
* **Maoz Tzur:** After lighting, it is customary to sing the 13th-century hymn *Maoz Tzur* ("Rock of Ages"). The song recounts Jewish survival through four exiles: Egypt, Babylon, Persia, and Greece, expressing hope for the final redemption.

### The Liturgy
* **Hallel:** We recite the complete **Hallel** (Psalms 113–118) on all eight days of Hanukkah. Unlike Passover, where the full Hallel is only said on the first days, the miracle of the Hanukkah oil is viewed as renewing itself fully each day, warranting complete and unbridled praise.
* **Al HaNissim:** A special paragraph of thanksgiving is added to the *Amidah* prayer and *Birkat Hamazon* (Grace After Meals). Interestingly, this text recounts the military victory ("You delivered the strong into the hands of the weak, the many into the hands of the few") but omits the miracle of the oil entirely, preserving the historical aspect of the holiday in prayer.
* **Torah Reading:** We read from the Book of Numbers (*Parashat Naso*), describing the gifts brought by the Princes (*Nesi'im*) for the dedication of the Tabernacle in the desert. This links the Maccabean "Rededication" back to the original "Dedication" of the Mishkan.

### Traditional Foods
To commemorate the miracle of the oil, it is customary to eat foods fried in oil.

* **Latkes (Levivot):** A staple of Ashkenazi cuisine, these are pancakes made from grated potatoes and onions, bound with egg and matzah meal, and deep-fried until golden and crispy. They are traditionally served with applesauce or sour cream.
* **Sufganiyot (Jelly Doughnuts):** The Israeli answer to the latke.
    * **The Standard:** A simple ball of yeast dough is deep-fried in bubbling oil until puffy and golden brown. It is then injected with sweet red strawberry jelly (*ribah*) and dusted with a blizzard of powdered sugar.
    * **The "Boutique" Revolution:** In recent years, bakeries in Israel and abroad have transformed the humble Sufganiyah into a gourmet canvas. In the weeks leading up to Hanukkah, store windows fill with "couture" doughnuts: doughs enriched with brioche or sourdough, filled with pistachio crème pâtissière, salted caramel, dulce de leche, or Belgian chocolate ganache. They are often topped with meringue spikes, crumbled cookies, gold leaf, or even served with small plastic syringes inserted into the dough, allowing the eater to inject an extra shot of liqueur or chocolate sauce at the moment of consumption.

### The Dreidel (Sevivon)
The Dreidel is a four-sided spinning top played with *gelt* (chocolate coins or nuts).
* **The Letters:** Each side bears a Hebrew letter: **Nun** ($\text{נ}$), **Gimel** ($\text{ג}$), **Hey** ($\text{ה}$), and **Shin** ($\text{ש}$). Together they form the acronym *Nes Gadol Haya Sham* ("A Great Miracle Happened There").
* **The Zionist Edit:** In Israel, the *Shin* is replaced by a **Pey** ($\text{פ}$) for *Po* ("Here"), changing the phrase to "A Great Miracle Happened **Here**."
* **The Game Rules:** Players put a coin in the pot and spin.
    * **Nun (*Nisht* - Nothing):** The player does nothing; the turn passes.
    * **Gimel (*Gantz* - All):** The player takes the entire pot. Everyone re-antes.
    * **Hey (*Halb* - Half):** The player takes half the pot.
    * **Shin (*Shtel* - Put in):** The player must put one coin into the pot.

## Part 4: Mystical Dimensions

*"It's not just about the past; it's about the cosmos."*

For the Jewish mystics, Hanukkah is not merely a historical commemoration but a cosmic event where the **Primordial Light** (*Or HaGanuz*) becomes accessible to the world.

### The Hidden Light
The **Bnei Yissaschar** (Rabbi Tzvi Elimelech of Dinov) teaches that the light we kindle is not ordinary fire. It is a spark of the light God created on the first day of Creation ("Let there be light"), which allowed Adam to see from one end of the universe to the other. God hid this light away (*ganuz*) for the righteous in the World to Come, but during the eight days of Hanukkah, it shines through the candles, allowing us to perceive the spiritual unity hidden within the physical world.

### The Power of 36
If one counts the total number of candles lit over the eight days (1+2+3+4+5+6+7+8), the sum is **36**. Kabbalists explain that this corresponds to the **36 hours** that the Primordial Light served Adam in the Garden of Eden before it was concealed. By lighting 36 candles, we actively reveal these sparks of hidden divine clarity. This also alludes to the *Lamed-Vav Tzadikim*, the 36 hidden righteous ones who sustain the world.

### The Eighth Dimension
The **Maharal of Prague** explains that numbers in Judaism have dimensions. The number **seven** represents the natural cycle of the physical world (seven days of creation, seven days of the week, seven musical notes). The number **eight**, however, represents that which is *above* nature—the metaphysical realm. Since the survival of the Jewish people and the burning of the oil were logically impossible, Hanukkah inhabits this "Eighth Dimension" of the supernatural.

### Zot Chanukah
The eighth and final day of Hanukkah is known as *Zot Chanukah* ("This is Hanukkah"). Mystics and Chassidic masters teach that this day is the final "seal" of the High Holiday season. While the books of judgment are opened on Rosh Hashanah and sealed on Yom Kippur, the "delivery" of the verdict is delayed until the last day of Hanukkah. It is considered a day of immense spiritual power for prayer and for giving *kvitelach* (prayer notes) to Rebbes.

## Part 5: Global Traditions

*"Oil and Courage: Traditions of the Diaspora."*

While the miracle occurred in Jerusalem, the light of Hanukkah spread to every corner of the globe, absorbing local flavors and customs along the way.

### North Africa: Sfinj & Chag HaBanot
In Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia, the oily treat of choice is **Sfinj**. Unlike the heavy, filled Sufganiyah, Sfinj is a rustic, unsweetened dough ring. It is deep-fried until chewy and golden, then dipped in sugar or honey to absorb the sweetness.
More significantly, the seventh night of Hanukkah is celebrated as **Chag HaBanot** ("The Festival of the Daughters"). It is a time to honor Jewish heroines like Judith and Hannah. Women refrain from work, eat dairy foods, and gather to reconcile old arguments. It is a night of female solidarity, dancing, and singing, celebrating the pivotal role women played in the redemption.

### Italy: Pollo Fritto
Italian Jews elevate the obligation to eat oil-rich foods to the main course. A classic Italian Hanukkah dish is **Pollo Fritto**—chicken pieces marinated in olive oil, lemon, and garlic, then dredged in flour and deep-fried to a golden crisp. This reflects the Italian Jewish approach of integrating the "miracle of oil" into the entire meal, not just the dessert.

### Kurdistan: The Eggshell Menorah
In the poorer communities of Kurdistan, children would save eggshells for weeks leading up to the holiday. On Hanukkah, they would carefully crack the tops, fill the empty shells with oil and a floating wick, and use them as a makeshift Menorah. This poignant custom highlights the devotion of a community that, despite poverty, found a way to beautify the mitzvah.

### India: Coconut Oil & Gulab Jamun
For the Bene Israel community of India, olive oil was scarce. They traditionally lit their Hanukkah lamps using **coconut oil**. Instead of latkes or doughnuts, their festive treat is **Gulab Jamun**—milk-solid balls deep-fried until dark gold and then soaked in a fragrant rose-water and cardamom syrup.

### Syria: The Extra Shamash
In Aleppo (Halab), a custom developed to light **two** *Shamashim* (or an extra candle) each night of Hanukkah. This was done as a specific act of gratitude for the community’s salvation from local persecutions, blending a local "Purim-style" deliverance into the national holiday.

## Tales

*"Legends of Light and Iron."*

### The Martyrdom: Hannah and Her Seven Sons
Recorded in the Talmud (Gittin 57b), this tragic account epitomizes the spiritual resistance of the era. A mother, Hannah, sees her seven sons arrested by the Emperor. One by one, they are commanded to bow to an idol, and one by one they refuse, quoting Torah verses before being executed. When the youngest is asked only to pick up the King’s ring to *appear* as if he bowed, he refuses:
> *"Woe to you, Caesar! If you care so much for your own honor, how much more must I care for the honor of the Holy One?"*

### The Heroine: Judith
The Midrash relates that during the siege of Bethulia, the widow Judith entered the enemy camp of the general Holofernes. She charmed him and fed him salty cheese to make him thirsty. When he drank wine and fell into a drunken stupor, she beheaded him with his own sword. She returned to her city with his head in a basket. The sight of their general’s head on the city walls caused the enemy army to flee in panic, lifting the siege.

### The Warrior: Eleazar and the Elephant
During the Battle of Beit Zechariah, Judah's brother Eleazar Avaran saw a massive war elephant decorated with royal armor. Believing King Antiochus to be riding it, he sacrificed himself to turn the tide of battle:
> *"He ran upon him courageously... and crept under the elephant, and thrust him from beneath, and slew him; and it fell to the earth upon him, and he died there."* —1 Maccabees 6:43-46

### The Pintele Yid
The Sfat Emet offers a Chassidic interpretation of the "pure flask of oil." The Greeks did not want to destroy the Jewish body, but to contaminate the Jewish soul with foreign philosophy. The miracle was not just that the oil burned, but that a single flask remained **sealed**—representing the *Pintele Yid*, the inner point of the Jewish soul that remains pure and inviolable, no matter how dark the world becomes.
