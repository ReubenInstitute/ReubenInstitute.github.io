## Introduction

Shavuot, known as the "Feast of Weeks", holds a unique dual significance within the Jewish calendar. It marks both the culmination of the spring harvest season in the Land of Israel and, more profoundly, the anniversary of the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai. This exploration will delve into its rich biblical and rabbinic foundations, trace its evolution through the ages, delve into its mystical interpretations, and highlight its diverse modern practices, offering a comprehensive journey into this pivotal Jewish holiday.

## Part 1: Foundations

_"So, what's up with all those names?"_

Shavuot is known by several distinct names, each showing a different aspect of its importance. These names highlight its basic connections to the agricultural cycles of the Land of Israel, its specific timing, and the historical observances related to the land's produce as commanded in the Torah.

### Chag HaShavuot (Feast of Weeks)
*Chag HaShavuot* gets its name from the seven-week count (*shavuot* meaning "weeks") commanded in the Torah. This count begins on the second day of Passover with the barley harvest (*Omer*) offering and lasts 49 days through the *Counting of the Omer* period, ending on the 50th day at Shavuot:

> _"You shall count off **seven weeks**; start counting the seven weeks when the sickle is first put to the standing grain. Then celebrate the **Feast of Weeks** to the Lord your God..."_ —Deuteronomy 16:9-10

This name shows the precise timing of the festival, marking the end of the Omer count and the transition to the wheat harvest season.

### Chag HaKatzir (Harvest Festival)
This name directly links Shavuot to the completion of the **wheat harvest** in ancient Israel. As the final major grain harvest of the spring agricultural cycle, it marked a key moment of offering gratitude for the land's produce. This connection is explicitly commanded in the Torah:

> _"You shall observe the **Feast of the Harvest**, of the first fruits of your labors, of what you sow in the field."_ —Exodus 23:16

The central ritual linked to this name was the offering of the **Two Loaves** (*Shtei HaLechem*) in the Temple. These loaves were made specially from the *new wheat* of the harvest and baked *leavened* (unlike the unleavened bread of Passover). As specified in Leviticus:

> *"You shall bring from your settlements two loaves of bread as a wave offering, made of two-tenths of an ephah of fine flour. They shall be baked with leaven, as first fruits to the Lord."* —Leviticus 23:17

These loaves were waved before the altar as a thanksgiving offering for the season's produce. This offering was crucial, as it allowed the bringing of other meal offerings from the new grain.

### Yom HaBikurim (Day of the First Fruits)
Shavuot was chosen as the first day for bringing *Bikurim* (first fruits) to the Temple. These consisted of the **Seven Species** typical of the Land of Israel. The name is directly related to the commandment:

> _"On the day of the **first-fruits**, when you offer a new meal-offering to the Lord, on your [Festival of] Weeks, it shall be a holy convocation to you."_ —Numbers 28:26

The *Bikurim* ritual had farmers select the **first and best ripe fruits** from these seven species. They would place them in baskets and carry them to the Temple in Jerusalem. Upon arrival, the farmer gave the basket to a priest.

> _"And now I bring the first of the fruit of the ground that You, Lord, have given me. You shall leave it before the Lord your God and bow low before the Lord your God."_ —Deuteronomy 26:10

This presentation, along with the recitation of Israel’s journey from Egypt to the promised land, officially began the season of bringing first fruits to the Sanctuary.

## Part 2: Evolution

_"Then, what changed since then?"_

With the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem in 70 CE, the direct agricultural observances of Shavuot, such as bringing the _Bikurim_, ceased. This pivotal moment led to a significant evolution in the holiday's focus, particularly for Jewish communities in the Diaspora. The holiday's agricultural roots shifted from physical offerings to symbolic expressions, while its rabbinic association with _Matan Torah_ (the Giving of the Torah) became increasingly central, ensuring its enduring relevance.

Although the Torah explicitly details Shavuot's agricultural aspects, it does not explicitly name it as the day the Torah was given. The connection was derived from the biblical account in Exodus:

> _"On the first day of the third month after the Israelites left Egypt—on that very day—they came to the Desert of Sinai."_ —Exodus 19:1

Based on this verse, the Rabbis calculated that the giving of the Torah occurred on the sixth day of Sivan, the date of Shavuot. This transformed the holiday from primarily an agricultural celebration to the "Time of the Giving of Our Torah" (_Z'man Matan Torateinu_).

The precise date of the Torah's giving is extensively discussed in the Talmud in Tractate Shabbat. The Talmud meticulously pieces together the sequence of events described in Exodus, starting from the Israelites' arrival at the wilderness of Sinai on the new moon (Rosh Chodesh Sivan) and the subsequent days of preparation.

While most Talmudic sages agree that the Torah was given on the **6th of Sivan** (the date of Shavuot), Rabbi Jose ben Halafta maintains that it was given on the **7th of Sivan**. The core of this debate often hinges on the interpretation of whether Moses added an extra day of preparation for the people, thereby shifting the revelation date by one day. The Talmud notes:

> _"[On the first of Sivan] Moses did not say anything at all to the Jewish people, since they were weary from the journey."_ —Talmud Bavli, Shabbat 86b.

This Talmudic discussion has implications for the observance of Shavuot. In Israel, Shavuot is observed on 6 Sivan. However, in the Jewish diaspora, a second day is added—a practice known as Yom tov sheni shel galuyot (Second-Day Yom Tov in the diaspora). Consequently, according to Rabbi Jose ben Halafta's calculation, it is only in the diaspora that Shavuot aligns with the day he believes the Torah was revealed.

The connection between Shavuot and Torah giving is underscored in the Talmud, where a discussion unfolds about how festivals should be observed. The Gemara presents a debate between Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua regarding the balance between spiritual pursuits (study) and physical enjoyment (eating and drinking) on a festival day. It was Rabbi Eleazar ben Pedat, a prominent 3rd-century CE sage, who interjected to make a specific point about Shavuot.

> _"Rabbi Elazar [Eleazar ben Pedat] said: 'All agree with regard to Atzeret [Hag HaShavuot] that [the element] of “for you” is required.' What is the reason? It is the day that the **Torah was given**."_ —Talmud Bavli, Pesachim 68b

## Part 3: Customs

Like other major holidays, Shavuot is a _Yom Tov_, a day of rest, meaning that traditional Jewish law prohibits work, similar to Shabbat, though with certain allowances for food preparation. As it is considered a joyous day, this is reflected in the omission of _Tachanun_ (supplicatory prayers) from the daily liturgy. This practice, common on festive days, underscores Shavuot's celebratory nature as a time of joy and gratitude for the Torah.

### Tikkun Leil Shavuot

This widespread all-night Torah study custom involves staying awake all night on the first night of Shavuot to study Torah. Its origin is linked to a Midrashic tradition that the Israelites overslept on the morning the Torah was to be given at Sinai, and Moses had to wake them.

> _"For the third day, God will come down in the sight of the people." Israel slept all through the night, because the sleep of Shavuot is pleasant and the night is short. Rabbi Yudan said: Not even a flea stung them. When the Holy One, Blessed Be God, came and found them asleep, he started to get them up with trumpets, as it is written: 'And it came to pass on the third day, when it was morning, that there were thunders and lightning.' (Exodus 19:16) And Moses roused Israel and took them to meet the Adonai our God, the Holy One, Blessed Be God, as it is written: 'And Moses brought forth the people [out of the camp] to meet God.' (Exodus 19:17)."_

This Midrashic account suggests that the Israelites, despite their anticipation, were overcome by sleep, necessitating divine intervention to awaken them for the momentous event. The custom of Tikkun Leil Shavuot is thus seen as a "rectification" (_tikkun_) for this historical oversight.

The Kabbalistic tradition, particularly as expressed in the Zohar, provides a mystical foundation for this all-night study. The Zohar portrays the act of studying Torah on Shavuot night as a way to "adorn" the _Shekhinah_ (Divine Presence) and the Jewish people, who are likened to a bride preparing for her wedding with God (the Torah). This imagery emphasizes the spiritual preparation and beautification achieved through Torah study.

> _"Happy is the portion of the Hevraya [Companions] when the **King** inquires of the **Matronita** [Queen/Bride] who has placed her jewels and illuminated her crown, and arranged her adornments? For there is none in the world who know how to array the jewels of the Bride other than the Hevraya. Happy is their portion in this world and the world that is coming."_ —Zohar,

The formalization of a specific study program for *Tikkun Leil Shavuot* is largely attributed to the 16th-century Kabbalists in Safed, particularly Rabbi Isaac Luria (the Arizal) and his disciple Rabbi Chaim Vital. Rabbi Chaim Vital, in his work *Sha'ar HaKavanot*, recorded the Arizal's structured approach to this all-night learning. This curriculum aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of Jewish wisdom and ensure that the night was spent in meaningful study, preparing the community for the spiritual "marriage" with the Torah on the following day.

The Arizal's program, as recorded by Rabbi Chaim Vital in *Sha'ar HaKavanot*, laid out a precise order of study. For instance, it specified beginning with *Parashat Bereishit* (the first Torah portion of Genesis) and reading its initial verses, then skipping to and reading its last three verses. This pattern was to be followed for every *parasha* (Torah portion) in the Torah. This structured approach, encompassing excerpts from every book of the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible), Mishnah, and Zohar, along with a listing of the 613 *mitzvot*, became the foundational text for the *Tikkun Leil Shavuot*.

One such instruction from the Arizal outlines this structured learning:

> _"This is the order of Scripture with which you should occupy yourself on this night in order to draw out the Keter as mentioned. Begin from Parashat Bereishit and read Parashat Bereishit until 'These are the generations of heaven and earth when they were created…' and afterwards skip and read the last three verses of Bereishit. From there on, read the first three and last three verses of every parasha. When you finish reading all the parshiot in…"_ —Sha'ar HaKavanot, Shavuot 1

This explicit instruction demonstrates the detailed and formalized nature of the study program established by the Arizal and documented by his student.

### Reading of the Book of Ruth

The Book of Ruth is traditionally read on Shavuot for several reasons. Its narrative unfolds during the barley and wheat harvest season, directly connecting to Shavuot's agricultural origins. Furthermore, Ruth's conversion to Judaism and her embrace of the Jewish people and their laws parallel the Israelites' acceptance of the Torah at Sinai. The book also traces the lineage of King David, who is traditionally believed to have been born and died on Shavuot.

#### Agricultural Connection

Machzor Vitry explains that the Book of Ruth is read on Shavuot because its narrative takes place during the harvest season.

> _"And we sit and read the Scroll of Ruth. [We do so] because it is written in it (Ruth 1:22), 'at the beginning of the barley harvest' - corresponding to [Shavuot], which is called, 'the first fruits of the wheat harvest' (Exodus 34:12)."_ —Machzor Vitry, Seder Shavuot 312

It explains that it is read on Shavuot also due to Ruth's conversion and acceptance of the Torah, which mirrors the Israelites' acceptance of the Torah at Mount Sinai.

> _"And some say it is because Ruth converted, as it is written (Ruth 2:12), 'under whose wings you have sought refuge'. And we say in [the chapter entitled] HaCholetz that Naomi said to her, 'It is forbidden for us to go outside of the Shabbat perimeter.' She said to her, 'Where you will go, I will go' (Ruth 1:16), etc. And the Israelites converted on [Shavuot] and entered under the wings of the Divine Presence with the receiving of the Torah"_ —Machzor Vitry, Seder Shavuot 312

This agricultural connection, highlighted by Machzor Vitry, is further emphasized by other prominent commentators. Rabbi David Abudraham, a significant medieval authority on Jewish liturgy, similarly points to the timing of the narrative within the harvest cycle as a primary reason for the Book of Ruth's reading on Shavuot. 

> _"...since in chapter one of Ruth it states: 'Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned from the fields of Moab-and they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest' — and Shavuot is also the time of the harvest, as it states: 'And you shall make for yourself a Festival of Weeks, the first of the wheat harvest (bikurei k'tzir chitim), it is therefore customary to read Ruth on Shavuot."_ —Rabbi David Abudraham, Sefer Abudraham

#### Torah Acceptance and Conversion

The reading of the Book of Ruth on Shavuot parallels Ruth's conversion with Israel's acceptance of the Torah at Sinai. Just as the Israelites embraced God's laws, Ruth, a Moabitess, committed to the Jewish people. Her acceptance, despite her Moabite origin, is clarified in the Talmud:

> _"They said to him: The halakha is: An Ammonite man is forbidden, but not an Ammonite woman; a Moabite man is forbidden, but not a Moabite woman."_ —Talmud Bavli, Yevamot 69a

This refers to the prohibition of marrying into the congregation of Israel, clarifying that it applies only to the male descendants of Ammon and Moab, not the females, thus permitting Ruth, a Moabitess, to convert and marry into the Jewish people. This understanding underscores the legal basis for her integration. The connection between Ruth's conversion and the giving of the Torah is further articulated by Rabbi Moshe Isserles, a prominent Ashkenazic halakhist, who succinctly states:

> _"we read Ruth on Shavuos because our ancestors who received the Torah converted, and so too Ruth converted"_ —Darchei Moshe (Rabbi Moshe Isserles)


#### Lineage of King David

Another significant reason for reading the Book of Ruth on Shavuot is its connection to King David. Tradition holds that King David was not only born but also died on the holiday of Shavuot.

> _"King David died on Shavuot"_ —Jerusalem Talmud, Chagigah, 2:3

Given this tradition, it follows that *Megilat Ruth*, which recounts the ancestry of David Hamelech (King David), is read on Shavuot. This emphasizes the divine providence in fulfilling the lives of the righteous, ensuring their years are complete from day to day.

> _"This comes to teach you that the Holy One, Blessed be He, sits and fills the years of the righteous from day to day and from month to month, as it is stated: 'The number of your days I will fulfill' (Exodus 23:26). Similarly, the Patriarchs merited that their years be fulfilled to the day, and so they died on the same date they were born."_ —Talmud Bavli, Rosh Hashanah 11a

####  Chesed (Loving-Kindness)

The Book of Ruth is also deeply rooted in the theme of *chesed*, or loving-kindness, a core value of the Torah itself. This intrinsic connection makes its reading particularly fitting for Shavuot, the holiday commemorating the giving of the Torah.

> _"Why is this scroll read during Shavuot? Because this scroll is wholly devoted to **chesed** and the Torah is all about **chesed**, as it is said, 'the law of kindness (*torat chesed*) is on her tongue' (Proverbs 31:26), and the Torah was given on the holiday of Shavuot"_ —Midrash Lekach Tov

The narrative of Ruth exemplifies acts of selfless kindness, as highlighted by rabbinic sages. Rabbi Ze'ira emphasizes that the primary purpose of the Book of Ruth is to teach about the profound reward awaiting those who engage in acts of loving-kindness.

> _"R. Ze'ira said: This scroll [of Ruth] tells us nothing of purity or impurity, of prohibition or permission. For what purpose was it written? To teach how great is the reward of those who do deeds of loving-kindness [chasadim]."_ —Midrash Ruth Rabbah 2:14

Furthermore, some interpretations connect the reading of Ruth on Shavuot to the idea that the Torah is acquired through humility and perseverance, often associated with "suffering and poverty." The story of Ruth, who endured hardship and chose a path of devotion, reflects this principle.

> _"What is the connection between Ruth and Atzeret [Shavuot], so that it is read on Atzeret, the time of the giving of the Torah? To teach you that the Torah is only given through suffering and poverty. And so it is written, 'Your tribe dwells there; O God, in Your goodness You provide for the needy' (Psalms 68:11)."_ —Midrash Ruth Zuta 1:1

This theme is echoed in other midrashic sources, reinforcing the idea that the spiritual depth of the Torah is often revealed through challenging circumstances and a humble spirit.

> _"What does the book of Ruth have to do with Shavuos? This [i.e. reading it on Shavuos] is to teach us that the Torah was only given through suffering and poverty."_ —Midrash Yalkut Shimoni 596

### Dairy Foods

_"And where did all that cheese come from?"_

A unique and beloved custom on Shavuot is the consumption of dairy foods, such as cheesecake, blintzes, and other milk-based dishes. This tradition, while not explicitly commanded in the Torah or Talmud, has evolved over centuries with various explanations rooted in Jewish thought and mysticism.

The earliest mention regarding the custom of eating dairy on Shavuot appears around 1270, by the medieval French commentator Rabbi Avigdor Tzarfati.

"The world asks why we eat bladan, and it seems that there is a hint in the Torah 'On the day of the first fruits, your Feast of Weeks, when you bring an offering of new grain to the Lord, you shall observe a sacred occasion…" -Rabbi Avigdor Tzarfati, commentary on the Torah

This indicates that by 1270, the custom of eating a dairy dish (possibly "bladan" or "fladen") on Shavuot was already established enough for people to be asking for its reason.

#### The Kashrut Revelation at Sinai

One prominent explanation for the custom of eating dairy on Shavuot links it to the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai. Upon receiving the Torah's intricate *kashrut* laws, including *shechita* and the separation of meat and milk, the Israelites found their existing meat and utensils non-kosher. As the revelation occurred on Shabbat, preventing immediate preparation of new kosher meat, they opted for readily available dairy. This commemorates their initial, impromptu kosher meal after Sinai.

Rabbi Israel Meir Kagan, known as the Chafetz Chaim, in his monumental work *Mishnah Berurah*, offers this explanation:

> _"When the Jews received the Torah at Mount Sinai, they were instructed to keep kosher dietary laws, including proper ritual slaughter (*shechitah*). All of their cooking utensils were rendered non-kosher, having been used improperly until then. Upon returning home after the Revelation, they had no choice but to eat dairy, since preparing kosher meat would take time. Thus, we eat dairy on Shavuot to remember this."_

#### Torah as Milk and Honey

Another widely cited reason for consuming dairy foods draws a parallel between the Torah and milk, often coupled with honey, as symbols of nourishment and sweetness.

The Land of Israel is described in Exodus as

> _"... a good and spacious land, a land flowing with **milk and honey** ..."_ —Exodus 3:8

This imagery, which appears numerous times in the Torah, signifies abundance, richness, and the inherent goodness of the land. 

The Song of Songs states:
> _"Your lips drip flowing honey, O bride; **honey and milk** are under your tongue, ..."_ —Song of Songs 4:11

Medieval commentators, such as Rashi, interpret this verse as referring to the words of Torah.

> _"'Your lips drip' - [interprets as] Explanations of Torah"_ —Rashi on Song of Songs 4:11

Just as milk provides complete sustenance for a nursing infant, the Torah offers spiritual nourishment essential for the soul's vitality and growth. This metaphor highlights the life-sustaining and wholesome nature of Torah's teachings. This comparison, particularly its association with milk and honey, is deeply embedded in Jewish tradition.


## Part 4: Mystical and Spiritual Dimensions

_"Is there like, a deeper, hidden meaning?"_

Beyond its historical and agricultural significance, Shavuot has been imbued with rich mystical and spiritual interpretations, particularly within Kabbalistic thought. These interpretations elevate the holiday to a cosmic event, emphasizing the profound relationship between God and the Jewish people.

### Diary

* Milk is seen as a symbol of purity in Kabbalistic thought, particularly in the Zohar, connecting to the purification of the Jewish people during the counting of the Omer leading up to Shavuot.
* A mystical and symbolic reason for the dairy custom stems from *gematria*, the Jewish system of assigning numerical values to Hebrew letters. The Hebrew word for milk, *chalav* (חלב), holds a numerical value of 40 (ח=8, ל=30, ב=2). This number directly corresponds to the 40 days Moses spent on Mount Sinai receiving the Torah from God, establishing a mnemonic and symbolic link between the food and the momentous event of the Torah's giving.
* A hint to dairy is found in the Shavuot offering verse. The initial letters of "חָדָ֔שׁ לַיהֹוָ֖ה בְּשָׁבֻעֹֽתֵיכֶם" (*chadashah la'Hashem b'Shavuotechem* – "new to the Lord on your Festival of Weeks") in Numbers 28:26 spell *chalav* (חלב – milk).
* Mount Sinai is callen *Har Gavnunim* ("mountain of majestic peaks"). The word *gavnunim* resembles *gevina* (cheese), with Kabbalists noting its "clean and pure" nature like cheese. 

### The Marriage Metaphor

In Kabbalah, the relationship between God and Israel is often described as a marriage. Shavuot, as the time of the giving of the Torah, is seen as the wedding day, with Mount Sinai serving as the _chuppah_ (wedding canopy). The Torah itself is considered the _ketubah_ (marriage contract), binding God and Israel in an eternal covenant. This metaphor transforms the legalistic act of receiving commandments into a profound spiritual union, as explicitly articulated in the Zohar:

> _"On this day [Shavuot], the Bride is united with her Groom... The Torah is the marriage contract (ketubah) between the Holy One and Israel... Mount Sinai was adorned like a bridal canopy (chuppah), and Moses was the attendant who led the Bride to her Groom."_ —Zohar III:98a, Emor

The Idra Rabba section elaborates on this mystical union, depicting Israel's transformation at Sinai through bridal imagery:

> _"At that moment, the Holy One, blessed be He, crowned Israel with holy crowns from above and below... and they were all adorned as a bride on her wedding day. Each was crowned with twenty-four adornments corresponding to the twenty-four books of Scripture... and the bride entered under the chuppah with her adornments."_ —Zohar III:127b, Idra Rabba

This intimate imagery is ritualized in some Eastern Sephardic communities and Hasidic groups through the ceremonial reading of a poetic _ketubah_ between God and Israel during Shavuot services. Composed by **Rabbi Israel ben Moses Najara (c. 1555–1625)**, a Safed-born kabbalist and liturgical poet, this document symbolically renews the covenantal bond established at Sinai.  

> _"Behold! The Bridegroom [God] has agreed to betroth the Bride [Israel]... pledging to cherish her with Torah and mitzvot... and the Bride has accepted His covenant."_ —Opening lines of Najara's Ketubah (Manuscript Ohel Moed, Venice 1607)


## Part 5: Global Traditions

_"What about other countries? Do they also eat cheesecake and learn all night?"_

Jewish communities around the world celebrate Shavuot with diverse customs and culinary traditions, reflecting their local cultures while maintaining the holiday's core themes.

### Ethiopians
The Ethiopian Jews, also known as Beta Israel, maintained a distinct form of Judaism for centuries, largely isolated from other Jewish communities. While historically speaking various languages like Qwaräñña and Tigrinya, today most Ethiopian Jews primarily speak Amharic. This isolation led to the development of unique customs and a calendar that differs in several aspects from mainstream Rabbinic Judaism. Their observance of Shavuot, known as **Bale Merr** (Feast of Weeks), traditionally falls on the **12th of Sivan**, unlike Rabbinic Judaism's 6th of Sivan. This difference stems from their interpretation of the Omer counting, which they begin after the entire Passover festival has concluded.

Central to Ethiopian Jewish practice is the **Orit**, their sacred scripture, written in Ge'ez, an ancient Ethiopian Semitic language precursor to modern Ethiopic languages like Amharic and Tigrinya. The Orit comprises the five books of Moses, plus Joshua, Judges, and Ruth. On Bale Merr, the *Kessim* (Ethiopian Jewish religious leaders) would read a longer portion from the Orit, particularly **Parashat Yitro** (the portion describing the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai). On the eve of Bale Merr, it was customary for everyone to immerse in a river and wear new clothes, preparing themselves to "receive the Torah anew".

The Ethiopian Jewish community also celebrates **Sigd**, a unique holiday observed on the 29th of Cheshvan, 50 days after Yom Kippur. This holiday, meaning "prostration" or "worship" in Ge'ez, parallels Shavuot in commemorating the renewal of the covenant between the Jewish people and God, and a yearning for Jerusalem and the rebuilding of the Temple. On Sigd, communities traditionally fast, ascend a high mountain, and the *Kessim* read from the Orit, symbolizing the re-acceptance of the Torah.


### Yemenites
Unlike many other Jewish communities, Yemenite Jews traditionally did not observe the custom of eating dairy foods on Shavuot. This was largely due to the specific halakhic traditions of their communities, the Baladi and the Shami. The Baladi community adhered to older Maimonidean rulings, while the Shami community was influenced by Sephardic and Kabbalistic elements. Neither group originally adopted the dairy practice, as it was a later development in other Jewish centers. However, many Yemenite Jews in Israel, particularly the younger generations, have now adopted this widespread custom and partake in dairy meals on Shavuot.

### Sephardic Jews

![El Pan de Siete Cielos](gfx/el-pan-de-siete-cielos.jpg) A unique Sephardic tradition, particularly among communities from Salonika, is the baking of "_Pan de Siete Cielos_" (Bread of Seven Heavens), a rich, milky challah often molded to represent Mount Sinai with seven rings of dough symbolizing the seven heavens. Some Sephardic communities also have a custom of reading a _Ketubah_ (marriage contract) between God and Israel, often composed by Rabbi Israel Najara, which is a poetic expression of the covenant. In some Sephardic communities, there's also a custom of splashing water on each other, symbolizing the Torah flowing like water.

### Moroccans
A unique custom among Moroccan Jews is the practice of splashing water on each other, which symbolizes the Torah flowing like water and is also believed to rouse individuals from slumber, reenacting the story of the Israelites needing to be awakened to receive the Torah at Sinai. Some also save pieces of matza from Passover, crush them, and mix them with milk and honey to eat after morning prayers, referencing the verse from Song of Songs.

### Persians
Persian Jews historically referred to Shavuot as "The Mo'ed of Flowers" (*Mo'ed Gol*), emphasizing the custom of decorating with greenery. They also make dishes like *Polao mastin* (rice and milk dish) and *koltcha shiri* (dairy cake).

### Iraqis
Iraqi Jews are known for making *sambusak*, a savory pastry filled with cheese, for Shavuot. They also have a tradition of *ka'hi*, a puff pastry dessert served with thick buffalo cream.

### Kurdistanis
Jews from Kurdistan traditionally eat a variety of dairy-based dishes for Shavuot. These include *glul* (a rice and milk porridge), *mdira* (a porridge made from crushed wheat and milk), *kutile* (dumplings with a buttery filling), and *kadi* (triangular pastries filled with cheese).

### Algerians
Historically, Algerian Jews prepared milky dishes and sweetened couscous for Shavuot. They would make their own cream cheese, yogurt, and sour milk a week before the holiday. A unique custom involved chanting a *piyyut* (liturgical poem) by Saadia Gaon on the Ten Commandments in Judeo-Arabic after the Torah reading. The tradition of splashing water on others was also present in Algerian Jewish communities, a custom now preserved in their sign language.

### Syrians
The ritual of reading the Rabbi Najara ketubah gained particular prominence in Aleppo and Damascus, where communities would physically process the decorated ketubah scroll around the synagogue—a practice still observed today in Syrian congregations worldwide. The ceremony embodies the Zohar's vision of Shavuot as the annual "renewal of the wedding vows" between God and Israel.

### Tripolitanians (Libyans and Tunisians)
![Kaak Bilsacar](gfx/kaak-bilsacar.jpg) Libyan and Tunisian Jewish communities have a charming custom of making special cookies called *Kaak Bilsacar* for Shavuot. These cookies are often shaped into symbolic forms that represent the stories of the Torah. For instance, they create Tablets, symbolizing the Tablets of the Law, and Ladders, which evoke Moses ascending Mount Sinai or Jacob's ladder. Birds are shaped to reference the silence at Sinai, while Hamsas are included for good luck. Other shapes include Eyeglasses, signifying the importance of Torah study, Keys as a symbol for sustenance, and Fish, representing fertility. These symbolic cookies are then strung together to form edible necklaces for children, offering a fun and educational way to engage them with the holiday's themes. The creation of Kaak Bilsacar is often a communal activity, with grandmothers frequently leading workshops for children and grandchildren in the lead-up to Shavuot, ensuring the tradition is passed down through generations.

## Part 6: Practice

Shavuot observance, like other Jewish holidays, follows a structured pattern of prayers, rituals, and customs that unfold chronologically. As a *Yom Tov* (Festival Day), it carries many of the same restrictions as Shabbat, prohibiting creative work, though with certain allowances for food preparation. The supplicatory prayers of *Tachanun* are omitted from the daily liturgy, reflecting the joyous nature of the holiday.

### Erev Shavuot (The Day Before Shavuot)

*   **Mincha (Afternoon Prayer):** The regular weekday *Mincha* service is recited, but *Tachanun* (supplicatory prayers) are omitted.
*   **Preparations:** Homes and synagogues are often decorated with flowers, greenery, and branches, symbolizing the harvest aspect of the holiday and the Midrashic tradition that Mount Sinai blossomed at the giving of the Torah.
*   **Candle Lighting:** Women and girls light holiday candles before sunset to usher in the holiday. The *Shehecheyanu* blessing is recited at this time.

### Shavuot Night (First Night of Shavuot)

*   **Maariv (Evening Prayer):** A festive holiday *Maariv* service is recited. It is customary in some communities to wait until full nightfall to begin *Maariv* on the first night of Shavuot, ensuring that the full 49 days of the Omer have been counted.
*   **Kiddush:** A special holiday *Kiddush* over wine is recited, including the *Shehecheyanu* blessing, marking the sanctity of the day.
*   **Festive Meal:** A festive holiday meal is eaten, traditionally including dairy foods such as cheesecake, blintzes, and other milk-based dishes.
*   **Tikkun Leil Shavuot:** Many communities observe the custom of staying awake all night to study Torah, known as *Tikkun Leil Shavuot*. This "rectification" aims to atone for the Israelites' supposed oversleeping before the giving of the Torah at Sinai. This study often includes excerpts from the entire Tanakh, Mishnah, and Zohar.

### Shavuot Day 1

*   **Shacharit (Morning Prayer):** The holiday *Shacharit* service is recited.
*   **Torah Reading:** Two Torah scrolls are removed from the Ark. The first reading is from Exodus 19:1-20:23, which recounts the revelation at Mount Sinai and the giving of the Ten Commandments. It is customary in many synagogues to stand when the Ten Commandments are read. The second reading (Maftir) is from Numbers 28:26-31, detailing the special offerings for Shavuot.
*   **Akdamut:** In many Ashkenazi communities, the liturgical poem *Akdamut* is recited before the Torah reading.
*   **Haftarah:** The Haftarah reading for the first day is from Ezekiel 1:1-28, 3:12, which describes the prophet's vision of the divine chariot, connecting to the revelation at Sinai.
*   **Musaf (Additional Prayer):** The *Musaf* service, an additional prayer recited on holidays, follows the Torah reading.
*   **Festive Meal:** Another festive meal is eaten.
*   **Mincha (Afternoon Prayer):** The holiday *Mincha* service is recited.

### Shavuot Night 2 (Diaspora Only)

*   **Candle Lighting:** In the Diaspora, a second day of Shavuot is observed. Holiday candles are lit after nightfall from an existing flame.
*   **Maariv & Kiddush:** The holiday *Maariv* service is recited, followed by *Kiddush* (without *Shehecheyanu*).
*   **Festive Meal:** A festive meal is eaten.

### Shavuot Day 2 (Diaspora Only)

*   **Shacharit:** The holiday *Shacharit* service is recited.
*   **Torah Reading:** The Torah reading for the second day is from Deuteronomy 15:19-16:17, which discusses the laws of the three pilgrimage festivals. If the second day falls on Shabbat, the reading is from Deuteronomy 14:22-16:17. The Maftir is again from Numbers 28:26-31.
*   **Reading of the Book of Ruth:** The Book of Ruth is traditionally read on Shavuot, often on the second day in the Diaspora.
*   **Yizkor:** The *Yizkor* (memorial) prayer is recited during the morning service, typically after the Torah reading, to remember departed loved ones. In Israel, where Shavuot is a one-day holiday, *Yizkor* is recited on the single day.
*   **Musaf:** The *Musaf* service is recited.
*   **Festive Meal:** A final festive meal is eaten.
*   **Mincha:** The holiday *Mincha* service is recited.

### Conclusion of Shavuot

*   **Havdalah:** The holiday concludes at nightfall with *Havdalah*, a ceremony marking the end of the sacred day and the return to the regular week. This ritual involves blessings over wine, spices, and a multi-wick candle, signifying the transition from the holy to the mundane.


## Tales

Jewish tradition is rich with stories that illuminate the spirit and significance of its holidays. These tales, often drawn from Midrash and other classical texts, offer deeper insights into the themes of Shavuot.

### The World Stood Still at Sinai

Midrashic tradition teaches that when God prepared to give the Torah at Mount Sinai, the entire world stood still. The birds ceased their chirping, the waves of the sea became calm, and no creature uttered a sound. All of creation held its breath, awaiting the divine pronouncement. This profound silence underscored the momentousness of the event, signifying that the entire universe was awaiting the revelation of God's will. It highlights the cosmic significance of the Torah, which provides the blueprint for creation itself.


> _"R. Berekhiah in the name of R. Levi said: At the hour when the Holy One, Blessed be He, said *I am the Lord your God* (Exodus 20:2), **no bird chirped, no bird flew, no ox lowed, the Ophanim (angelic wheels) did not tremble, the wind did not blow, thunder did not roar, lightning did not flash, the Seraphim did not say *Holy*, the ministering angels did not lift their wings to say *Blessed is the glory of the Lord from His place* (Ezekiel 3:12)... The entire world was altogether silent and hushed, waiting expectantly for the sound of the utterance: *I am the Lord your God*."_ —Pesikta de-Rav Kahana 12:24

> _"At the hour when the Holy One, Blessed be He, said *I am the Lord your God*... **the entire world trembled and the ministering angels became silent from their song**, as it is said (Psalms 77:19) *The sound of Your thunder was in the whirlwind; the lightning lit up the world; the earth trembled and shook*... R. Yohanan said: It was so overwhelming for them that **they swooned and their souls departed**, as it is said (Song of Songs 5:6) *My soul departed when he spoke*."_ —Exodus Rabbah 29:9

> _"R. Yohanan said: At the hour when the Torah was given to Israel, **no bird chirped, no bird flew, no ox lowed, the Ophanim did not fly, the Seraphim did not say *Holy*, the sea did not roar, creatures did not speak**. Rather, the world was silent and hushed, and the voice went forth: *I am the Lord your God*."_ —Midrash Tehillim 68:8

> _"At the hour when the Holy One, Blessed be He, said *I am the Lord your God*, the heavens and the earth trembled, and the earth was convulsing like a bee, and the mountains and hills were crumbling... **And everything became silent, even the ministering angels; and no bird chirped, no bird flew, no ox lowed, no Ophanim fluttered, no Seraphim said *Holy***. Rather, they were all silent. And why? Because the Torah was being given."_ —Midrash Tanchuma, Va'etchanan 3


### Moses and the Angels

When Moses ascended to heaven to receive the Torah, the angels protested. They argued before God, "What is man, that You are mindful of him? What is the son of man, that You take note of him? Give the Torah to us, for we are pure and holy, and we have served You faithfully since creation!"

God instructed Moses to respond to their challenge. Moses, with divine guidance, asked the angels: "Did you go down to Egypt? Were you enslaved by Pharaoh? Did you cross the Red Sea? Did you witness the plagues? Do you have parents to honor, or children to teach? Do you have fields to work, or poor to support? Is there jealousy among you, or evil inclination?"

The angels, having no experience of human struggles, suffering, or the complexities of earthly life, fell silent. Moses then declared, "The Torah was given for humanity, for those who live in a world of challenge and choice, where its laws can guide and elevate them." Convinced, the angels embraced Moses and even taught him some secrets of the Torah. This tale emphasizes that the Torah was given specifically for humanity, to navigate the realities of the physical world and to uplift human existence.


> When Moses ascended on high, the ministering angels said before the Holy One, blessed be He:  
> **"Master of the Universe! What is a mortal doing among us?"**  
> He answered them: **"He has come to receive the Torah!"**  
> They protested before Him: **"This treasured object hidden with You for nine hundred seventy-two generations before the creation of the world—You wish to give it to flesh and blood?  
> *‘What is man that You are mindful of him, and the son of man that You take note of him?’"***  
>   
> The Holy One, blessed be He, said to Moses: **"Answer them!"**  
> He said before Him: **"Master of the Universe, I fear they may consume me with the breath of their mouths!"**  
> He said to him: **"Hold onto the Throne of Glory and answer them!"**  
>   
> He said before them:  
> **"It is written in the Torah: *‘I am the Lord your God who brought you out of Egypt’*—Did you go down to Egypt? Were you enslaved to Pharaoh?**  
> **It is written: *‘You shall have no other gods’*—Do you dwell in a world of idolatry?**  
> **It is written: *‘Remember the Sabbath day’*—Do you perform labor requiring rest?**  
> **It is written: *‘Honor your father and mother’*—Do you have a father and mother?**

### The Akdamut Poem

The *_Akdamut_* poem, recited for centuries by Ashkenazi Jews before the Shavuot Torah reading, carries a legendary origin rooted in Jewish resilience. Tradition attributes it to **Rabbi Meir bar Yitzchak of Worms** (11th century), who composed it amid brutal persecution. Forced into religious debates with Christian theologians, Rabbi Meir defended Judaism with such eloquence and conviction that he fortified his community’s faith. His 90-line Aramaic masterpiece became a shield against conversion pressures—a bold declaration that Jews would cling to Torah "even when crushed like potter’s clay."

The poem begins by dwarfing creation itself: were all oceans ink, skies parchment, and every being a scribe, they could never capture God’s greatness. It then confronts exile’s darkness head-on, naming Torah the eternal "light" and "banner" for Israel. In a powerful pivot to Sinai, it recalls the hushed heavens and open skies as God gave laws to "free the captive." Finally, it envisions a messianic dawn where truth prevails, the righteous "glow like stars," and peace reigns. Through 90 meticulously structured lines, *_Akdamut_* binds Jewish suffering to unwavering hope—proving faith’s triumph over tyranny.
