## Introduction

*"A new year for trees? Like a birthday party?"*

Tu B'Shevat, often referred to as the "New Year for Trees," holds a unique position within the Jewish calendar. This booklet delves into its rich history, halakhic foundations, mystical interpretations, and modern practices, offering a comprehensive journey into this fascinating Jewish holiday.

## Part 1: Foundations

*"So, how it all started, anyway?"*

Tu B'Shevat's initial significance is rooted primarily in the rabbinic legal discussions concerning agricultural practices in the Land of Israel. Its designation as a "New Year" stems from its role in determining the age of trees and the calculation of various tithes.

Central to the agricultural laws of ancient Israel were the concepts of **tithing (*ma'aserot*)** and the prohibition of **fruit from young trees (*orlah*)**. These laws ensured the sanctity of the land's produce and supported the priestly class and the poor.

The fruit of a newly planted tree is forbidden for consumption for its first three years:

> *"When you enter the land and plant any tree for food, you shall regard its fruit as forbidden. Three years it shall be forbidden for you, not to be eaten."* —Leviticus 19:23

In the fourth year, the fruit, known as *neta reva'i* (fourth-year fruit), was holy and had to be brought to Jerusalem and eaten there, or redeemed with money.

> *"In the fourth year all its fruit must be consecrated as a praise offering to the LORD."* —Leviticus 19:24

Rashi (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, 11th century), in his commentary on the Torah, highlights the underlying purpose of the *orlah* prohibition. Rashi cites a Midrash where Rabbi Akiva says that the mitzvah of orlah helps increase future produce and productivity:

> *"To increase your produce.' This mitzvah that you will observe will increase your produce, and its reward is that I will bless your fruits and saplings."* —Rashi, citing Midrash

Additionally, the Torah commands the separation of various **tithes (*ma'aserot*)** from agricultural produce. These tithes served different purposes.

**Ma'aser Rishon** (the First Tithe), is a biblical commandment requiring the giving of one-tenth of agricultural produce to the Levites for their sustenance, as they did not receive a land inheritance.

> *"To the children of Levi, behold, I have given all the tithe in Israel for an inheritance, in return for their service which they perform, the service of the Tent of Meeting."* —Numbers 18:21

> *"For the tithe of the children of Israel, which they set apart as a heave offering to the Lord, I have given to the Levites for an inheritance; therefore I have said to them, 'Among the children of Israel they shall have no inheritance.'"* —Numbers 18:24

**Ma'aser Sheni** (the Second Tithe), is a tithe set aside in the first, second, fourth, and fifth years of the seven-year agricultural cycle. It was to be brought to Jerusalem and consumed there by the owner, or redeemed for money and the money spent in Jerusalem. The Torah commands:

> *"You shall surely tithe all the produce of your seed that comes out of the field year by year. And you shall eat before the Lord your God, in the place which He will choose to make His name dwell there, the tithe of your grain, of your new wine, and of your oil, and the firstborn of your herds and of your flocks, so that you may learn to fear the Lord your God always."* —Deuteronomy 14:22-23

**Ma'aser Ani** (the Poor Tithe), was given in the third and sixth years of the agricultural cycle instead of *Ma'aser Sheni*. The Torah specifies:

> *"At the end of every three years you shall bring forth all the tithe of your increase in that year and shall lay it up within your gates. And the Levite, because he has no portion nor inheritance with you, and the sojourner, and the orphan, and the widow, who are within your gates, shall come and eat and be satisfied, so that the Lord your God may bless you in all the work of your hand which you do."* —Deuteronomy 14:28-29

The bounty of the Land of Israel and its produce are often celebrated in prophetic texts, underscoring the divine blessing associated with the land's fruitfulness:

> *"Do not be afraid, land; be glad and rejoice. For the Lord has done great things. Do not be afraid, you wild animals, for the pastures in the wilderness are becoming green. The trees are bearing their fruit; the fig tree and the vine yield their riches. Be glad, people of Zion, rejoice in the Lord your God, for he has given you the autumn rains because he is faithful. He sends you abundant showers, both autumn and spring rains, as before. The threshing floors will be filled with grain; the vats will overflow with new wine and oil."* —Joel 2:21-24

The precise timing for calculating these agricultural obligations was crucial for farmers. The Mishnah, in Tractate Rosh Hashanah, enumerates four distinct "New Years" within the Jewish calendar, each serving a different legal or agricultural purpose. Among these, the Mishnah identifies a specific date for trees:

> *"There are four New Years: The first of Nisan is the New Year for kings and for festivals. The first of Elul is the New Year for the tithing of animals. Rabbi Elazar and Rabbi Shimon say: the first of Tishrei. The first of Tishrei is the New Year for years, for *shemittah* (sabbatical) and *yovel* (jubilee) years, for planting, and for vegetables. The first of Shevat is the New Year for trees, according to the words of Beit Shammai; Beit Hillel says: on the fifteenth of that month."* —Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 1:1

This establishes Tu B'Shevat (the fifteenth of Shevat) as the accepted date for the "New Year for trees," following the opinion of Beit Hillel. Early commentators elucidate the practical implications of this date. Rabbi Ovadiah Bartenura (15th century), in his commentary on this Mishnah, explains its significance for tithing:

> *"New year for the tree – for the matter of tithing of fruits, for we don't tithe fruits of the tree that formed prior to Shevat on [fruit] that formed after Shevat, for with regard to a tree, we go after the formation of the fruit."* —Bartenura on Mishnah Rosh Hashanah

An early divergence emerged among Karaite communities, who rejected rabbinic interpretation and adhered strictly to Beit Shammai's position, observing the New Year for Trees on the first of Shevat as a literal reading of the Mishnaic alternative. The primary halakhic (Jewish legal) implications of this date were twofold:

Firstly, it marked the cut-off point for calculating the age of a fruit tree for the purpose of *orlah*. The Talmud, discussing the implications of the New Year for trees, explicitly connects this prohibition to Tu B'Shevat. It clarifies that the fruit's status for *orlah* and *neta reva'i* is determined by this date:

> *"And if the planting, layering, or grafting took place more than thirty days before Rosh HaShana, the fruit of this planting is prohibited until the fifteenth of Shevat of the fourth year since the tree's planting, even though the three years were already completed the previous Rosh HaShana. This principle applies both for *orla* during the year of *orla*, when it is prohibited to eat the fruit, and for fourth-year produce during the year of fourth-year produce, which must be eaten in Jerusalem or redeemed before it is eaten outside Jerusalem."* —Talmud Rosh Hashanah 10a, 20

This means that if a tree was planted before Tu B'Shevat, that year would count as its first year of growth for *orlah* purposes, even if it was planted only a short time before the fifteenth of Shevat. If planted after Tu B'Shevat, that year would not count, and the *orlah* count would begin the following year. As the Talmud further notes:

> *"Determining the date of the New Year for trees is crucial for the laws of *orlah*, which forbid the deriving of any benefit from the fruit for the first three years."* —Talmud Rosh Hashanah 14a, 1

Secondly, Tu B'Shevat served as the new year for the tithing of fruits (*ma'aserot*). All fruits that blossomed before Tu B'Shevat were considered part of the previous year's harvest for tithing purposes, while those that blossomed after Tu B'Shevat were considered part of the new year's harvest. This distinction was crucial for farmers to correctly separate the required tithes from their produce. The timing was particularly relevant because by the middle of Shevat, the majority of the winter rains have fallen, and the sap has begun to rise in the trees, signaling the start of the new growing season in the Land of Israel.

## Part 2: Evolution

*"Wait! If it's about Israel, then why do we still celebrate it in other places?"*

While Tu B'Shevat's origins were rooted in the practicalities of agricultural law in the Land of Israel, its significance began to evolve, particularly for Jewish communities living in the Diaspora. Despite the diminishing direct halakhic relevance for those outside the Land, Tu B'Shevat did not disappear; instead, it "survived and took on fresh meaning in the Middle Ages," transforming into a minor holiday with a more symbolic and spiritual character.

### Diaspora's Impact

For Jewish communities living in the Diaspora, the practical halakhic applications of *orlah* and *ma'aserot* were generally not directly applicable. This irrelevance manifested differently across communities: Ethiopian Jews (Beta Israel) preserved a direct connection by interpreting *orlah* through their agricultural cycle. Based on Leviticus 19:23's prohibition against early fruit consumption, they ate barley bread and sprouted greens on Tu B'Shevat – symbolizing both renewal and the 'permissible' first fruits after the three-year *orlah* waiting period. For the vast majority of Jews scattered across the globe, however, the date lost its immediate functional relevance.

Despite this, the memory and concept of Tu B'Shevat persisted as a symbolic connection to the Land of Israel and its unique agricultural cycle. This absence of direct legal imperative allowed the holiday to become fertile ground for new interpretations and practices, enabling its evolution from a purely fiscal or agricultural new year into a day with broader spiritual and communal significance. This shift laid the groundwork for the later mystical and celebratory dimensions that would come to define Tu B'Shevat, transforming it from an obscure legal date into a cherished tradition.

### Festive Hint: No Tachanun

*"So, it's a happy day?"*

An early and significant indicator of this shift towards festivity is the halakhic practice of omitting *Tachanun* (supplicatory prayers) on Tu B'Shevat. *Tachanun* is typically recited on weekdays as an expression of humility and a plea for divine mercy, but it is omitted on joyous or semi-festive days. The absence of *Tachanun* on Tu B'Shevat signifies that, despite not being a major biblical holiday, it carries a measure of inherent joy and serves as a day of celebration rather than supplication. This practice was codified in Jewish law, with some rabbinic authorities as early as the 10th century forbidding fasting on Tu B'Shevat, recognizing its celebratory nature. For instance, the 14th-century halakhic compilation *Kol Bo* states:

> "And on the fifteenth of Shevat, one does not say Tachanun, and one does not fast."

This subtle halakhic directive set a tone of minor festivity for the day.

### Connection to the Land

Beyond the omission of *Tachanun*, the memory and concept of Tu B'Shevat persisted as a vital symbolic connection to the Land of Israel and its unique agricultural cycle for Diaspora Jews. Even without direct involvement in the agricultural laws, the day provided an opportunity to acknowledge the renewal of nature and express gratitude for the produce of the earth. This allowed communities to maintain a spiritual bond with the land from afar, fostering a sense of continuity and heritage.

A key aspect of this connection is the emphasis on the "Seven Species" (*Shivat HaMinim*) for which the Land of Israel is praised in the Torah:

> "A land of wheat and barley, and vines and fig trees and pomegranates; a land of olive oil and honey." —Deuteronomy 8:8

These specific agricultural products served as a tangible link to the bounty and holiness of the Promised Land, each carrying unique significance and practical uses in ancient Israel:
*   **Wheat** was a primary staple food, essential for making bread and symbolizing sustenance.
*   **Barley**, ripening earlier, was a robust crop often used for animal feed.
*   **Grapes** were cultivated for wine, a central element in many Jewish rituals, and also consumed fresh or dried, symbolizing joy and fruitfulness.
*   **Figs** were a common fruit, eaten fresh or dried, and symbolize abundance, wisdom, and fertility, often serving as a metaphor for peace and prosperity.
*   **Pomegranates**, with their numerous seeds, are traditionally associated with the 613 *mitzvot* (commandments) of the Torah, fertility, and beauty.
*   **Olives** were crucial for producing olive oil, used for food, symbolizing peace, wisdom, and vitality, and are deeply connected to the land.
*   **Honey**, which refers to date honey (*silan*), a significant source of sweetness and sustenance, symbolizing the abundance and royalty of the land. 

### Fruit-Eating Customs

*"And where did all the fruit-eating come from?"*

As the practical agricultural relevance diminished, the holiday became fertile ground for new interpretations and customs. A key development was the emergence of the custom of eating fruits on Tu B'Shevat. In the Middle Ages, a "minor ceremony of eating fruits" began to be observed, aligning with the Mishnaic description of the day as a "New Year". In the mid-16th century, *Sefer HaMachzor HaGadol* writes:

> "Since Tu B'Shevat is the beginning of the year for trees, the custom is for everyone to buy on this day a little of every available fruit."

A century later, in the late 17th century, the Magen Avraham (Rabbi Avraham Gombiner) also writes:

> "It is the custom on Tu b'Shevat to eat many different kinds of fruit." —Magen Avraham, Notes on Code of Law (131:16)

Further solidifying this practice, Rabbi Chaim Palagi, writing in the 19th century in his work *Moaid L'kol Chai*, observes:

> "...the night of Tu B'Shevat is the beginning of the year for trees, and most Jews are accustomed to arrange the table with all the fruits of trees and the earth, as much as one can afford and each person recites a bracha on one fruit..."

This practice was a direct way for Diaspora Jews to affirm their allegiance to the Land of Israel and its bounty. It became customary to partake in fruits, particularly those from the Seven Species. In the European Diaspora, where fresh fruits were not readily available in winter, dried fruits such as raisins, figs, and carobs became especially popular and continue to be a cherished tradition. This custom served as a tangible link to the agricultural heritage of the Land of Israel, transforming the day from a purely legalistic marker into a celebration of nature's renewal and a symbolic expression of connection to the Jewish homeland.


## Part 3: Mystical Rebirth

*" So you say there are hidden meanings?"*

The medieval custom of eating fruits – established as a tangible link to Israel's agricultural heritage – provided fertile ground for Kabbalistic innovation. But how did a day primarily concerned with agricultural taxes transform into a deep spiritual experience, complete with a Seder and mystical meditations?

While Tu B'Shevat's origins were rooted in the practicalities of agricultural law, its most profound transformation occurred in the 16th century, primarily through the work of the Kabbalists in Safed (Tzfat), in the Land of Israel. These Jewish mystics, particularly Rabbi Isaac Luria (the Ari) and his disciples, imbued the day with rich spiritual meaning, shifting its focus from an agricultural new year to a cosmic celebration of the "Tree of Life" (*Etz Chaim*) and a vehicle for spiritual rectification (*tikun*).

### The Four Worlds

*"And suddenly we have four cups of wine, four kinds of  everything. Where did that number come from?"*

In Kabbalah, the universe is understood through the concept of the "Four Worlds" (*Olamot*)—**Atzilut** (Emanation), **Beriah** (Creation), **Yetzirah** (Formation), and **Assiyah** (Action)—which represent descending levels of divine manifestation. These worlds progress from the most spiritual to the most physical, each embodying a different degree of divine revelation and concealment. This hierarchical arrangement is a fundamental aspect of Kabbalistic cosmology.

| World                  | Meaning and Symbolism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| :--------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Atzilut (Emanation)    | Represents the highest level of divine manifestation, pure spiritual essence, and closeness to the divine. It is the most spiritual and unhindered revelation.                                                                                                |
| Beriah (Creation)      | Corresponds to a level where divine revelation is more overt and unhindered, a step down from pure emanation but still highly spiritual.                                                                                                                     |
| Yetzirah (Formation)   | Symbolizes a stage where divine light is more accessible than in Assiyah, but still contains a core of hiddenness. It's a level of formation and structure.                                                                                                  |
| Assiyah (Action)       | Represents the most physical and lowest level of divine manifestation, where the divine essence is most concealed and requires effort to access. It is the world of action and material existence.                                                              |

For the Kabbalists, the physical world was a reflection of higher spiritual realms, and trees served as a powerful metaphor for the divine emanations. They believed that through eating fruits with the proper intention, one could elevate "sparks of holiness" (*nitzotzot*) that had become dispersed throughout creation. This act was seen as a way to repair the cosmic order and even rectify the primordial sin of Adam and Eve, who partook of the forbidden fruit. Thus, Tu B'Shevat became an opportune time for spiritual growth and connection to the divine flow of beneficence.


### The Tu B'Shevat Seder

This mystical celebration of Tu B'Shevat was formalized in a foundational text known as *Pri Etz Hadar* ("Fruit of the Majestic Tree"). While the exact author is debated, it emerged from the school of Isaac Luria and was first published as a pamphlet in Venice in 1728. This text provided the blueprint for the Tu B'Shevat Seder, outlining a structured ritual meal that mirrored aspects of the Passover Seder, complete with specific fruits, four cups of wine, and mystical readings.

The *Pri Etz Hadar* and subsequent Kabbalistic teachings structured the Seder around the concept of the "Four Worlds" (*Olamot*), as described above. Each stage of the Seder corresponds to one of these worlds, progressing from the most spiritual to the most physical.

### Fruits

The Seder incorporates various types of fruits, each symbolizing a different aspect of these worlds and the spiritual journey. Typically, fruits are categorized based on their physical structure:

| Fruit Type                                                                                                | Examples                                     | World                  |
| :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :--------------------- |
| Hard, inedible outer shell but an edible interior                                                         | Pomegranates, walnuts, almonds               | Assiyah (Action)       |
| Soft, edible exterior but an inedible pit                                                                 | Dates, olives, peaches                       | Yetzirah (Formation)   |
| Entirely edible                                                                                           | Figs, grapes                                 | Beriah (Creation)      |
| Fragrant, non-edible items (sometimes representing pure spiritual essence, not always a distinct edible fruit) | Etrog                                        | Atzilut (Emanation)    |

### Four Cups of Wine

Similar to the Passover Seder, four cups of wine are consumed, with their color gradually shifting from white to red. This progression symbolizes the transition from the dormant, colorless state of winter to the vibrant, life-filled hues of spring and the increasing revelation of divine light in the world. Each cup is accompanied by readings and meditations appropriate to the spiritual world it represents, fostering a deeper connection to the cycles of nature and the divine.

| Wine Color Association | World                  |
| :--------------------- | :--------------------- |
| Pure white             | Atzilut (Emanation)    |
| White with red hint    | Beriah (Creation)      |
| Rosé/pink              | Yetzirah (Formation)   |
| Deep red               | Assiyah (Action)       |

The Tu B'Shevat Seder, as developed by the Kabbalists, is a communal ritual meal involving the consumption of various fruits and four cups of wine, interspersed with readings from biblical, rabbinic, and Zoharic texts. It provides a framework for participants to engage with the mystical dimensions of the holiday, meditating on the symbolism of the fruits and wine, and striving for spiritual elevation and rectification through conscious consumption and study. This comprehensive approach transformed Tu B'Shevat from a minor legal observance into a vibrant spiritual tradition, deeply cherished in many Jewish communities today.


## Part 4: Modern Times

*"So, what now?"*

In modern times, the Tu B'Shevat Seder, originally a mystical practice, has become the most prominent form of observance, offering a tangible way for many Jews to connect with the holiday's themes of renewal, gratitude for nature, and the Land of Israel. While often adapting Kabbalistic interpretations for broader accessibility, these Seders maintain core elements such as the consumption of a wide array of fruits, including the Seven Species and new fruits, often categorized by their physical structure (shells, pits, or entirely edible). Central to the Seder are also the four cups of wine, which progressively shift in color from white to red, symbolizing the awakening of nature and a spiritual journey. These elements are accompanied by a rich tapestry of readings from biblical, rabbinic, and mystical texts, alongside contemporary reflections, fostering meditations on growth, the cycle of life, and the human connection to nature.

**Tree Planting** Perhaps the most direct and iconic modern practice associated with Tu B'Shevat is tree planting. This can involve planting trees locally in one's own community or donating to organizations that plant trees in Israel. This act directly connects to the holiday's agricultural roots and its emphasis on renewal and stewardship of the land. It serves as a tangible expression of the enduring bond between the Jewish people and the Land of Israel, recalling its ancient agricultural cycles and supporting its flourishing in modern times. For many, it became a day to express solidarity with the Zionist movement and to support the reforestation efforts in Israel.

**Environmental Activism** Tu B'Shevat has become a significant day for Jewish environmentalism. Many Jewish organizations and synagogues use the day to educate about ecological issues, promote sustainable practices, and engage in advocacy for environmental protection. This practice is deeply rooted in the Jewish value of *shomrei adamah* (guardians of the earth) and *tikkun olam* (repairing the world). The Torah's prohibition against wanton destruction, particularly of fruit-bearing trees during wartime (Deuteronomy 20:19-20), known as *Bal Tashchit*, is a foundational principle of Jewish environmental ethics, highlighted on this day. It underscores the Jewish teaching that humanity is entrusted with the care of creation, not its exploitation.

**Learning and Study** The day is also dedicated to learning about the natural world, Jewish texts related to nature, and the history and evolution of Tu B'Shevat itself. Educational programs, lectures, and discussions often take place in synagogues, schools, and community centers. A central symbolic theme explored is the comparison of human beings to trees, rooted in biblical verses like Deuteronomy 20:19: "For man is like the tree of the field." This metaphor prompts introspection on personal growth, with roots representing heritage, the trunk signifying character, branches symbolizing actions, and fruit embodying good deeds and positive impact. Tu B'Shevat encourages personal renewal, mirroring the rejuvenation of nature.

### Global Traditions

*"What about other places? What do they do?"*

Communities worldwide incorporate local elements into their Tu B'Shevat observances while maintaining the core symbolism of the holiday. Below are some examples of how various Jewish communities around the globe celebrate Tu B'Shevat, reflecting their unique cultural and historical contexts:

**Bukharan Jews:** In the Bukharan Jewish tradition, Tu B'Shevat Seders often feature a wide array of dried fruits, including apricots and mulberries. While the holiday was sometimes known as "the day of eating the seven species" in Bukhara, it was customary for Bukharan Jews to consume as many as 30 different types of fruit, symbolizing the bounty of the Land of Israel and the spiritual elevation associated with the holiday. The emphasis on dried fruits reflects their historical availability in the Diaspora, particularly in regions where fresh produce was scarce during the winter months when Tu B'Shevat falls. This practice aligns with the Kabbalistic understanding of elevating "sparks of holiness" through the consumption of fruits, making the diverse array of dried fruits a tangible link to the land's produce and its spiritual significance.

**Moroccan Jews:** Moroccan Jewish communities celebrate Tu B'Shevat with unique culinary customs, including the serving of *sfinge* (fried dough) with honey. *Sfinge* are free-form doughnuts, originating from Muslim Andalusia, which spread throughout the Maghreb region. Although often associated with Hanukkah due to their preparation in oil, these sweet treats are also part of Moroccan Jewish culinary traditions for other festive occasions. Drizzling them with honey further enhances their sweetness and connects them to the biblical description of the Land of Israel as a "land flowing with milk and honey." This custom highlights the adaptation of local foods into Jewish holiday observances, creating a distinct regional flavor for the celebration.

**Indian Jews (Bene Israel):** For Indian Jews, particularly the Bene Israel community, Tu B'Shevat is marked by a special ceremony called *malida*. This ritual, also observed for other significant life events like *b'nai mitzvah*, centers around a unique rice dish, often prepared with pressed rice flakes (*poha*) and enriched with local ingredients such as coconut, cardamom, almonds, and pistachios. The *malida* is typically presented on a round platter, with the rice dish mounded to resemble Mount Sinai, surrounded by fresh fruits and flowers. The prominent use of coconut and mango in their celebrations stems from the abundance of these fruits in India, allowing the community to integrate local agricultural produce into their observance of the "New Year for Trees."

**Persian Jews:** Persian Jewish traditions for Tu B'Shevat include the recitation of special *piyyutim*, which are liturgical poems or hymns, reflecting a rich poetic and spiritual engagement with the holiday. While the Kabbalistic Seder gained prominence, the composition and inclusion of *piyyutim* for occasions like Tu B'Shevat date back centuries, with some found in the Cairo Genizah from as early as the 15th century. Beyond the formal liturgy, Persian Jews also developed a distinctive communal custom: climbing onto neighbors' roofs and lowering empty baskets down chimneys, expecting them to be returned laden with fruit. This playful yet symbolic act underscores the communal joy and the sharing of the land's bounty, transforming the holiday into a vibrant, interactive celebration of nature's renewal and interconnectedness.

**Ashkenazi Jews:** In many Ashkenazi communities, a common practice is to eat a variety of fruits, often including 15 different kinds, accompanied by the recitation of psalms. Some within the Hasidic community have a custom of pickling or candying the etrog (citron) from Sukkot and consuming it on Tu B'Shevat, often with a prayer that they will be worthy of a beautiful etrog for the following Sukkot. In the Eastern European Diaspora such as Russia, where fresh fruits were not readily available in winter, dried fruits such as carobs (known in Yiddish as "bokser") were particularly popular. A specific custom among Jews in Russia was the recitation of the *Shehecheyanu* blessing over carobs. A well-known Chabad anecdote recounts the Baal Shem Tov miraculously causing an orange orchard to appear in the middle of a Russian winter for Tu B'Shevat, emphasizing the spiritual importance of celebrating the holiday with its traditional fruits even in challenging environments.

**Iraqi Jews:** Iraqi Jewish communities developed a tradition of consuming a minimum of 100 different types of fruits, nuts, grains, and vegetables on Tu B'Shevat, symbolizing the abundant produce of the Land of Israel.

**Yemeni Jews:** While Yemeni Jews observed Tu B'Shevat, their practices generally aligned with broader Sephardic and Mizrahi traditions of consuming fruits, particularly the Seven Species. Unlike some communities where the Kabbalistic Tu B'Shevat Seder became a central feature, its adoption in Yemen was more nuanced. While Kabbalah did influence some segments of Yemeni Jewry, particularly among the "Shami" faction, the "Baladi" faction, exemplified by the Darda'im movement, often emphasized a return to earlier, more rationalistic traditions and opposed the widespread integration of Kabbalah into public practice. This internal dynamic likely contributed to the absence of highly distinct and elaborate Tu B'Shevat Seder customs compared to communities where Kabbalah was more universally embraced in public observance.

**Jews in the Southern Hemisphere:** For Jewish communities located in the Southern Hemisphere (such as in South Africa, Australia, or Argentina), Tu B'Shevat falls during their summer season. This contrasts with the winter setting in the Northern Hemisphere where the holiday originated, leading to celebrations that often include outdoor activities, ice cream, and cold watermelon.

**Ethiopian Jews (Beta Israel):** Historically, the ancient Jewish community of Ethiopia, known as Beta Israel, did not celebrate Tu B'Shevat *as a major holiday* in the same way as rabbinic Judaism, along with other holidays not explicitly mentioned in the Torah, such as Hanukkah or Purim. However, they did observe a practice related to the *orlah* prohibition on this date, eating barley bread and sprouted greens as a symbolic act of renewal and permissible first fruits after the three-year waiting period, reflecting their direct connection to agricultural cycles.

## Final Thoughs

The evolution of Tu B'Shevat from an obscure legal date to a widely celebrated holiday, encompassing mystical depth, environmental consciousness, and communal festivity, highlights its remarkable adaptability and enduring relevance in contemporary Jewish life.

In modern times, Tu B'Shevat has continued to adapt, becoming a widely celebrated holiday that embraces a spectrum of observances. For many, the Tu B'Shevat Seder, with its focus on fruits, wine, and meaningful readings, offers a tangible way to connect with Jewish heritage and the natural world. Beyond the Seder, the holiday inspires tree planting, environmental advocacy, and a renewed commitment to our role as guardians of creation.

Ultimately, Tu B'Shevat stands as a vibrant example of how Jewish tradition can evolve while remaining deeply rooted in its foundational texts and values. It is a day that celebrates the renewal of nature, the enduring connection to the Land of Israel, and the perpetual human quest for spiritual growth and responsibility. It is a reminder that, like the trees, we too are part of a continuous cycle of growth, renewal, and the bearing of good fruit in the world.

## Tales

Jewish tradition is rich with stories, parables, and legendary accounts that serve to illustrate concepts, transmit values, and connect generations. These stories, drawn from different periods of Jewish history, offer traditional perspectives that complement the historical and halakhic discussions of the holiday.

### Honi the Circle-Drawer and the Carob Tree

The story of Honi the Circle-Drawer, a 1st-century BCE Jewish scholar, is a well-known narrative from Jewish texts, detailing his seventy-year sleep and his encounter with a carob tree. 

> The Sages taught: It happened that Honi HaMe'agel was distressed all his days about this verse: "A Song of Ascents: When the Lord brought back those who returned to Zion, we were like dreamers" (Psalms 126:1). He said: Is it possible for a person to sleep for seventy years?

> One day he was walking on the road and saw a certain man planting a carob tree. Honi said to him: "How many years until it bears fruit?" The man replied: "Seventy years." Honi said to him: "Is it obvious to you that you will live seventy years, that you expect to benefit from this tree?" The man replied: "I found the world filled with carob trees. Just as my ancestors planted for me, so too I plant for my descendants."

> Honi sat down to eat bread, and sleep overcame him. A cliff formed around him, and he slept for seventy years. When he awoke, he saw a certain man gathering fruit from that tree. Honi said to him: "Are you the one who planted it?" The man replied: "I am his son's son." Honi said to him: "If so, then it is clear that I have indeed slept for seventy years."

> He went to his house and said to them: "Is the son of Honi HaMe'agel still alive?" They said to him: "His son is no longer alive, but his grandson is here." He said to them: "I am Honi HaMe'agel." They did not believe him. He went to the study hall and heard the Sages discussing him, saying: "The teaching of Honi HaMe'agel is difficult for us." He said to them: "I am Honi HaMe'agel." They did not believe him, nor did they show him the respect he deserved. He became disheartened, prayed for mercy, and died.

—Talmud Bavli, Tractate Ta'anit 23a.


> תָּנוּ רַבָּנַן: מַעֲשֶׂה בְּחוֹנִי הַמְעַגֵּל שֶׁהָיָה מִצְטַעֵר כָּל יָמָיו עַל מִקְרָא זֶה: "שִׁיר הַמַּעֲלוֹת בְּשׁוּב ה' אֶת שִׁיבַת צִיּוֹן הָיִינוּ כְּחֹלְמִים" (תהלים קכו, א). אָמַר: מִי אִיכָּא דְּנָאֵים שִׁבְעִין שְׁנִין?

> יוֹמָא חַד הֲוָה אָזֵיל בְּאוֹרְחָא, חֲזָא הַהוּא גַּבְרָא דְּקָא נָטַע חָרוּבָא. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: עַד כַּמָּה שְׁנִין טָעֵין? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: עַד שִׁבְעִין שְׁנִין. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: פְּשִׁיטָא לָךְ דְּחָיֵית שִׁבְעִין שְׁנִין? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אֲנָא עָלְמָא בְּחָרוּבָא אַשְׁכַּחְתֵּיהּ, כִּי הֵיכִי דִּשְׁתַלוּ לִי אֲבָהָתִי — שְׁתַלִי נָמֵי לִבְרָאִי.

> יָתֵיב חֲצִיף לַחְמָא, נָמֵי לֵיהּ שִׁינְתָּא. אִיתְכְּרַךְ עֲלֵיהּ צוּקָא, וְנָם שִׁבְעִין שְׁנִין. כִּי קָם, חֲזָא הַהוּא גַּבְרָא דְּקָא לָקֵט מִינֵיהּ. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אַתְּ הוּא דִּשְׁתַלְתְּהּ? אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אֲנָא בְּרֵיהּ דְּבַר בְּרֵיהּ אֲנָא. אֲמַר לֵיהּ: אִם כֵּן, וַדַּאי נָמֵי שִׁבְעִין שְׁנִין.

> אָזֵיל לְבֵיתֵיהּ, אֲמַר לְהוּ: בְּרֵיהּ דְּחוֹנִי הַמְעַגֵּל קָאֵי. אֲמַר לְהוּ: אֲנָא נִיהוּ. לָא הֵימְנוּהוּ. אָזֵיל לְבֵית הַמִּדְרָשׁ, שָׁמַע לְהוּ לְרַבָּנַן דְּקָא מְדַּכְּרִי לֵיהּ: קַשְׁיָא לַן שְׁמַעְתֵּיהּ דְּחוֹנִי הַמְעַגֵּל. אֲמַר לְהוּ: אֲנָא נִיהוּ. לָא הֵימְנוּהוּ, וְלָא עָבְדִי לֵיהּ יְקָרָא כִּדְמִבְּעֵי לֵיהּ. חֲלַשׁ דַּעְתֵּיהּ, בְּעָא רַחֲמֵי, וּמִית.


### The Baal Shem Tov and the Orange Orchard

The tale of the Baal Shem Tov, the founder of Hasidic Judaism, and the miraculous orange orchard in Russia is a classic within Hasidic tradition, primarily found in **"Shivchei HaBesht" (In Praise of the Baal Shem Tov)**, first published in 1815.

> It once happened that on Tu B'Shevat, the Baal Shem Tov was traveling with his disciples and arrived at a place where there were no fruits at all. The Baal Shem Tov was very distressed that he had no fruits to make blessings over on Tu B'Shevat, as was the custom. He said to his disciples: "Come, let us go to a certain place." They traveled, and behold, they arrived at a large orchard full of oranges, even though it was the midst of a harsh Russian winter.

> The disciples were very surprised, as it was impossible for an orange orchard to grow in such a place and at such a time. The Baal Shem Tov said to them: "Come, let us make blessings over the fruits and enjoy them." And they made blessings and ate with great joy.

> Afterward, they returned to their place. One of the disciples, Rabbi Moshe who was with him, returned to the place of the orchard, but found nothing there, only snow and ice. He returned and asked the Baal Shem Tov: "Rebbe, what was there? Was it a dream?"

> The Baal Shem Tov replied: "Since I had great distress that there were no fruits for Tu B'Shevat, and I wanted to fulfill the custom completely, I brought, through holy power, an orchard from the Land of Israel here, so that we could make blessings and eat. And when the need was fulfilled, it returned to its place."

> מעשה שהיה, פעם אחת בט"ו בשבט, היה הבעל שם טוב נוסע עם תלמידיו, והגיעו למקום שאין שם פירות כלל. והיה הבעל שם טוב מצטער מאוד, שאין לו פירות לברך עליהם בט"ו בשבט כנהוג. אמר לתלמידיו: "בואו ונלך למקום פלוני." ונסעו, והנה הגיעו לפרדס גדול מלא תפוזים, אף על פי שהיה זה בחורף רוסי קשה.

> התלמידים התפלאו מאוד, שכן לא ייתכן שפרדס תפוזים יצמח במקום כזה ובזמן כזה. אמר להם הבעל שם טוב: "בואו ונברך על הפירות, ונהנה מהם." ובירכו ואכלו בשמחה גדולה.

> לאחר מכן, חזרו למקומם. אחד התלמידים, רבי משה שהיה עמו, חזר למקום הפרדס, אך לא מצא שם דבר, רק שלג וקרח. חזר ושאל את הבעל שם טוב: "רבי, מה היה שם? האם זה היה חלום?"

> אמר לו הבעל שם טוב: "כיוון שהיה לי צער גדול על שלא היו פירות לט"ו בשבט, ואני רוצה לקיים את המנהג בשלמות, הבאתי בכוח הקדושה פרדס מארץ ישראל לכאן, כדי שנוכל לברך ולאכול. וכשנגמר הצורך, חזר למקומו."
