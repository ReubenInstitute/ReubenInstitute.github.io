## Introduction

*"A holiday where we dress up, make noise, and get drunk? Is this even Jewish?"*

Purim, known as the "Feast of Lots," is arguably the most joyous and exuberant holiday in the Jewish calendar. It commemorates the miraculous salvation of the Jewish people in ancient Persia from a genocidal plot orchestrated by Haman, the wicked vizier to King Ahashverosh. This booklet will delve into its dramatic biblical origins, trace its development through rabbinic decrees, explore its profound mystical interpretations, and highlight its diverse modern practices, offering a comprehensive journey into this pivotal Jewish holiday.

## Part 1: Foundations

*"So, where does this wild story even come from?"*

Purim's origins are vividly recounted in the biblical **Book of Esther** (Megillat Esther), one of the Five Scrolls (Megillot) within the Ketuvim (Writings) section of the Hebrew Bible. The narrative unfolds in the vast Persian Empire during the reign of King Ahashverosh (King Ahasuerus, traditionally identified with King Xerxes I, in the 5th century BCE.

The story begins with King Ahashverosh deposing his Queen Vashti for disobedience. A grand beauty pageant is held to find a new queen, and a young Jewish orphan named Hadassah, known by her Persian name Esther, is chosen, concealing her Jewish identity under the guidance of her cousin and guardian, Mordechai.

The villain is Haman, an Agagite (from Agag, the king of the Amalekites), a people who were long-standing and bitter enemies of the Israelites. Haman rises to become the king's chief minister. Enraged by Mordechai's refusal to bow down to him, Haman plots not only Mordechai's death but also the annihilation of all Jews throughout the empire.

Haman casts lots to determine the auspicious date for this extermination. The word *pur* (plural *purim*) is a Persian word meaning "lot" or "dice," referring to an ancient method of selection, similar to drawing straws or rolling dice. The lot cast by Haman falls on the 13th day of the Hebrew month of Adar.

> *"In the first month, that is, the month of Nisan, in the twelfth year of King Ahashverosh, pur (which means 'the lot') was cast before Haman concerning every day and every month, [until it fell on] the twelfth month, that is, the month of Adar."* —Esther 3:7

A royal decree is issued, authorizing the massacre. Upon learning of the decree, Mordechai urges Esther to intercede with the king, reminding her that she may have attained her royal position for this very purpose. Risking her life by approaching the king unbidden, Esther invites Ahashverosh and Haman to two banquets. At the second banquet, Esther dramatically reveals her Jewish identity and exposes Haman's wicked plot to exterminate her people.

> *"Then Queen Esther answered and said, 'If I have found favor in your sight, O king, and if it pleases the king, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my request. For we have been sold, I and my people, to be destroyed, to be slain, and to perish. But if we had been sold as male and female slaves, I would have held my tongue, although the enemy could not compensate for the king’s damage.' Then King Ahashverosh answered and said to Queen Esther, 'Who is he, and where is he, who dares presume in his heart to do such a thing?' And Esther said, 'The adversary and enemy is this wicked Haman!'"* —Esther 7:3-6

Enraged, the king orders Haman to be hanged on the very gallows Haman had prepared for Mordechai.

> *"Then said Harbonah, one of the chamberlains, before the king, Behold also, the gallows fifty cubits high, which Haman had made for Mordecai, who spake good for the king, standeth in the house of Haman. Then the king said, Hang him thereon."* —Esther 7:9

New decrees are issued, empowering the Jews to defend themselves against their enemies.

> *"The king's edict granted the Jews in every city the right to assemble and protect themselves; to destroy, kill and annihilate the armed men of any nationality or province who might attack them and their women and children, and to plunder the property of their enemies."* —Esther 8:11

On the 13th of Adar, the Jews successfully defend themselves, and on the 14th, they rest and celebrate. In Shushan, the capital, the fighting continues for an additional day, and the celebration occurs on the 15th of Adar.

The holiday is named Purim, plural of *pur*, meaning "lot," in reference to the lots Haman cast to determine the date of the Jews' destruction.


## Part 2: Customs

*"Why do we do all these weird things like dressing up and getting drunk? And how did this become a holiday anyway?"*

While the Book of Esther recounts the events, the establishment of Purim as an annual holiday with specific observances was solidified through rabbinic decree. The Megillah itself concludes with Mordechai and Esther instructing the Jews to observe these days annually.

> *"Mordecai wrote down these events. And he sent letters to all the Jews in all the provinces of King Ahashverosh, both near and far, enjoining them to observe the fourteenth day of the month of Adar and also the fifteenth day of the same month, year after year, as the days on which the Jews gained relief from their enemies, and as the month which was turned for them from sorrow into gladness and from mourning into a holiday; that they should make them days of feasting and gladness, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor."* —Esther 9:20-22

Building upon these foundational instructions from Mordecai and Esther, particularly the call for "days of feasting and gladness, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor," Purim's celebration was further structured around four central observances, each with its primary rabbinic sources: Kriat Megillah, which involves reading the Book of Esther both during the day and at night; Mishloach Manot, the sending of food gifts to friends and neighbors; Matanot La'Evyonim, the giving of gifts to those in need; and Seudat Purim, a festive meal that includes a directive to drink.

### 1. Kriat Megillah (Reading the Megillah)

The obligation to read the Megillah twice – once at night and again during the day – is a fundamental aspect of Purim observance. This practice is rooted in rabbinic decree, as detailed in the Talmud:

> *"A person is obligated to read the Megillah at night and repeat it during the day."* —Talmud Bavli, Megillah 4a

This obligation is further codified in later Jewish law, confirming the dual reading requirement:

> *"A person is obligated to read the Megillah at night and return to repeat it during the day."* —Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 687:1

### 2. Mishloach Manot (Sending Food Gifts)

The custom of sending food gifts to friends and neighbors on Purim day is derived from the Book of Esther itself, which describes the holiday as days of "sending portions one to another."

> *"to make them days of feasting and gladness, and of sending portions one to another, and gifts to the poor."* —Megillat Esther 9:22

Rabbinic texts further elaborate on the specific requirements for fulfilling this commandment, clarifying that the sending of "portions one to another" means at least two portions to a single recipient.

> *"Rav Yosef taught: 'and sending portions one to another' – two portions to one person."* —Talmud Bavli, Megillah 7a

This specific detail is upheld in later Jewish legal codes, which confirm the obligation to send two distinct items to at least one friend.

> *"One is obligated to send to his friend two portions of meat or types of food, as it is stated 'and sending portions one to another' – two portions to one person."* —Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 695:4

### 3. Matanot La'Evyonim (Gifts to the Poor)

Giving charity to the needy on Purim is a direct biblical injunction, as indicated in the Book of Esther:

> *"and gifts to the poor."* —Megillat Esther 9:22

The Sages further defined this commandment, specifying that the phrase "gifts to the poor" implies giving at least two gifts to at least two individuals.

> *"'and gifts to the poor' – two gifts to two people."* —Talmud Bavli, Megillah 7a

This ensures that the spirit of generosity and inclusion is met, as codified by Maimonides:

> *"One is obligated to distribute to the poor on Purim day. Not less than two poor people; one gives to each one gift, either money or types of cooked food or types of edibles, as it is stated "and gifts to the poor" – two gifts to two poor people."* —Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Megillah 2:16

### 4. Seudat Purim (Festive Meal)

The festive meal on Purim day is a central part of the celebration, emphasizing joy and gladness, as indicated in the Book of Esther:

> *"days of feasting and gladness."* —Megillat Esther 9:22

Rabbinic tradition includes a unique directive regarding the consumption of wine during this meal, urging a level of intoxication that blurs distinctions.

> *"A person is obligated to become intoxicated on Purim until he cannot distinguish between 'Cursed be Haman' and 'Blessed be Mordechai'."* —Talmud Bavli, Megillah 7b

This directive, known as *Ad d'lo yada*, is a hallmark of the Purim feast and is reiterated in Jewish law:

> *"A person is obligated to become intoxicated on Purim until he cannot distinguish between 'Cursed be Haman' and 'Blessed be Mordechai'."* —Shulchan Aruch, Orach Chayim 695:2

### Costumes and Masks

The widespread and joyous custom of wearing costumes and masks on Purim, while seemingly a modern innovation, has deep historical roots and rich symbolic meaning within Jewish tradition. It contributes significantly to the carnival-like atmosphere of the holiday.

The practice of masquerading in costumes and wearing masks on Purim is relatively recent by Jewish historical standards, with its earliest mentions appearing in the early 14th century in Italy. Rabbi Kalonymus, a scholar from Provence, is noted for an early reference to this custom. Some scholars suggest that the practice may have been influenced by the Roman Carnivals or Mardi Gras traditions, which often coincided with Purim on the calendar. These festivals, known for their elaborate parades and masquerade balls, might have inspired Jewish communities in Italy to adopt similar festive expressions.

Initially, the custom, particularly cross-dressing, faced some consternation among rabbinic authorities due to concerns about transgressing biblical prohibitions (Deuteronomy 25:2). However, rabbis like Yehuda Minz, a 15th-century Italian rabbi, argued that costume-wearing, including cross-dressing, was permissible when done for the purpose of creating joy, as it was clearly in jest and not for immoral reasons. This more lenient view ultimately prevailed, and the custom spread from Italy across Europe, reaching Middle Eastern countries by the 19th century.

**Reasons and Symbolism**
While not explicitly mentioned in early classical sources like the Talmud, later authorities and commentators have offered several insightful explanations for the custom of wearing costumes and masks:

1.  **The Hidden Hand of God (_Hester Panim_)**: One of the most profound reasons is the symbolic representation of God's hidden presence in the Purim story. Unlike other biblical narratives, the Book of Esther does not explicitly mention God's name, and the salvation of the Jewish people appears to unfold through a series of seemingly natural coincidences rather than overt miracles. The name "Esther" itself is linked to the Hebrew word _hester_ (הֶסְתֵּר), meaning "hidden" or "concealment," further emphasizing this theme. By hiding behind masks and costumes, participants metaphorically remind themselves that even when God seems hidden, He is actively orchestrating events behind the scenes.

2.  **Esther's Hidden Identity**: The custom also commemorates Queen Esther's initial concealment of her Jewish identity from King Ahashverosh. She "masked" who she truly was until the opportune moment to reveal herself and save her people.

3.  **Reversal (_Venahafoch Hu_)**: The theme of "reversal" is central to Purim, where the Jews' fate was dramatically turned from impending destruction to triumph. Wearing costumes, which transform a person's appearance, reflects this profound reversal of fortune – from victim to victor.

4.  **Anonymity for Charity**: A practical reason suggests that costumes allow those giving or receiving charity on Purim to maintain anonymity and dignity. Since it is a custom to give to "anyone who sticks his hand out" without inquiry, masks could help preserve the modesty of those in need.

5.  **Theatricality and Purim Spiels**: The development of Purim spiels (humorous plays retelling the Purim story) in the 17th century further popularized the use of costumes and allegory, creating a sense of "comedic catharsis."

6.  **Mordechai's Royal Garb**: Some interpretations connect the custom to Mordechai's transformation from wearing sackcloth and ashes to royal robes, symbolizing a change in status and fortune.

7.  **Gentiles Feigning Jewishness**: Another theory suggests that costumes commemorate the non-Jews who, after the reversal of Haman's decree, feigned Jewishness to curry favor.

**Modern Practice**
Today, wearing costumes on Purim remains a vibrant and beloved tradition, particularly popular among children, who eagerly participate in dressing up as characters from the Purim story or other figures. The holiday provides an engaging way for them to connect with Jewish history and values.

## Ta'anit Esther (The Fast of Esther)

*"What's up with the fast the day before?"*

The joyous revelry of Purim is preceded by a more somber observance: Ta'anit Esther, the Fast of Esther. Observed on the 13th of Adar, the day before Purim, this minor fast day serves as a spiritual preparation for the holiday, recalling the prayers and fasting that preceded the miraculous salvation of the Jewish people.

The primary inspiration for Ta'anit Esther comes directly from the Book of Esther itself. When Mordechai informed Esther of Haman's decree to annihilate the Jews, Esther famously instructed him:

> *"Go, gather together all the Jews that are present in Shushan, and fast ye for me, and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day; I also and my maidens will fast in like manner; and so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law; and if I perish, I perish."* —Esther 4:16

This three-day fast, undertaken by Esther and the Jews of Shushan, was a desperate plea for divine intervention before Esther risked her life by approaching King Ahashverosh unbidden. It is generally understood that this original fast took place in the month of Nisan, around the time of Passover, not Adar.

However, the Fast of Esther observed today on the 13th of Adar is not explicitly mentioned in the Mishnah or Talmud. Its earliest references appear in rabbinic literature from the Geonic period (around the 8th century CE). Rav Ahai Gaon, a leading scholar of this era,is one of the earliest sources to reference the day.

> *"The thirteenth [of Adar] is a time of gathering/community for all... What is 'gathering'? A fast day. And what is 'day of entry/gathering'? That they gather in it and sit in fast and seek mercy."* —Sheiltot of Rav Ahai Gaon, 67

This passage clarifies that the 13th of Adar is designated as a day for the entire community to come together, specifically for the purpose of fasting and offering prayers for mercy.

Rabbinic authorities have offered various interpretations for the establishment of this fast on the 13th of Adar.

### Commemoration of Esther's Original Fast

Many commentators, including some Rishonim (medieval rabbinic authorities), connect the Fast of Esther to the three-day fast initiated by Queen Esther. The Kol Bo explains that:

> *"the fast is in commemoration of the fast that Ester decreed on the Jews of Shushan."* —Kol Bo, Siman 62

The Shibbolei HaLeket cites Rashi as explaining that:

> *"Ta'anit Esther commemorates the three-day fast observed by the Jews of Shushan at Esther's behest during the month of Nisan."*

The date was likely shifted from Nisan to Adar to avoid fasting during the festive month of Passover, as:

> *"Even though those fasts were in the days of Passover, the practice is to make the fast right before Purim."* —Shibbolei HaLeket

### Commemoration of the Jews' Battle Fast

Other opinions, notably Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh, suggest that the fast on the 13th of Adar commemorates the day the Jews gathered to defend themselves against their enemies. Rabbeinu Tam states that:

> *"they must have fasted before going to war just as the Midrash says that the Jews fasted before the war with Amalek in the desert."* —Rabbeinu Tam

The Shiltot writes that:

> *"the 13th of Adar because that was when the Jews gathered together to fast and pray prior to going to war in the days of Mordechai and Ester."* —Shiltot, Shiltah 67

The Rosh writes:

> *"It is a day of gathering for everyone” – that everyone gathers together for the Fast of Esther. The rural population comes to the cities to recite Seliḥot and supplications, just as on this day the Jews gathered together to defend themselves and thus required divine mercy."* —Rosh

### "Custom" vs. a "Decree"

There has been discussion among rabbinic figures regarding the exact halakhic status of Ta'anit Esther. Some, like Rashi, viewed it primarily as a widespread custom (*minhag*). The Machzor Vitri writes:

> *"neither of Torah origin nor of Rabbinic origin, rather the nation conducted itself thus."* —Machzor Vitri 245

Rashi describes this fast as a "mere custom" (*minhag be'alma*) and criticizes those who treat it with unnecessary stringency. Others, such as the Raavad and Rambam, considered it a rabbinic obligation, deriving it from the verse in Esther 9:31, which mentions the Jews accepting "the matter of the fasts." The Raavad asserts that:

> *"In addition, we have a written reference to it as it says: 'To confirm these days of Purim in their appointed times, as Mordecai the Jew and Queen Esther had enjoined them, and as they had ordained for themselves and for their seed, the matters of the FASTINGS and their cry…' – in other words, to observe this fast each and every year."* —Raavad

The Rambam (Hilchot Taaniot 5:5) also cites Esther 9:31 as the source for observing Ta'anit Esther. This difference in classification can influence the level of stringency applied to its observance.


## Part 3: Mystical and Spiritual Dimensions

*"Is there like, a deeper, hidden meaning to all this chaos and celebration?"*

Beyond the historical narrative, Purim has been imbued with rich mystical and spiritual interpretations, particularly within Kabbalistic thought.

### The Hidden Hand of God

One of the most striking features of the Book of Esther is the complete absence of God's name. Unlike other biblical books, there is no explicit mention of divine intervention. Kabbalists and commentators explain this as a central theme of Purim: God's presence is *hester panim*, "the hiding of the Face." The miracle of Purim was not an overt, supernatural event like the splitting of the Red Sea, but rather a series of seemingly natural coincidences and reversals that, in retrospect, reveal a hidden divine hand guiding events. The very name "Esther" is linked to the Hebrew word *hester*, meaning "hidden" or "concealment." This teaches that even when God seems hidden, He is actively involved in the world, orchestrating salvation through natural means and human agency.

### Reversal and Transformation

The theme of "reversal" (*venahafoch hu* – "it was turned upside down") is central to Purim. Haman's plot to destroy the Jews is reversed, leading to his downfall and the elevation of Mordechai and Esther. This symbolizes the transformative power of divine providence, turning sorrow into joy and mourning into festivity. Mystically, this reversal suggests that even the darkest situations can be transformed into opportunities for profound spiritual growth and revelation.

### _Ad d'lo yada_ and Spiritual Intoxication

The custom of drinking *Ad d'lo yada* (until one cannot distinguish between "Blessed be Mordecai" and "Cursed be Haman") is often interpreted mystically. It is not merely about physical intoxication, but about transcending rational thought and experiencing a state of spiritual unity where the distinctions between good and evil, revealed and hidden, temporarily blur. This state allows for a deeper appreciation of God's hidden presence and the underlying unity of all existence, even in seemingly contradictory events.

## Part 4: Modern Times

*"So, what do people do now? Is it still just parties and costumes?"*

In modern times, Purim continues to be a vibrant and multifaceted holiday, blending ancient traditions with contemporary expressions. While the emphasis on joy and the four *mitzvot* remains paramount, the holiday also serves as a day for community, creative expression, and continued reflection on themes of survival and hidden miracles.

**Community Celebrations:** Synagogues and Jewish community centers worldwide host public Megillah readings, often accompanied by lively parties, costume contests, and carnivals for children. These events foster a strong sense of community and shared celebration.

**_Purim Spiel_ (Purim Play):** The *Purim Spiel* is a humorous play or skit that retells the Purim story, often with contemporary references, satire, and musical numbers. Originating in medieval Europe, these plays have evolved into a beloved tradition, allowing for creative expression and lighthearted mockery, sometimes even of religious figures or community leaders.

**_Adloyada_ Parades:** In Israel, particularly in cities like Tel Aviv and and Holon, large, colorful parades known as *Adloyada* are held, featuring elaborate floats, costumes, music, and dancing. These carnivalesque processions draw large crowds and embody the exuberant spirit of Purim.

**Charity and Unity:** The *mitzvot* of *Mishloach Manot* and *Matanot La'Evyonim* continue to emphasize the importance of social responsibility, mutual support, and unity within the Jewish community. Many communities organize collective efforts to prepare and distribute food packages and ensure that funds reach those in need.

**Children's Involvement:** Purim is particularly beloved by children, who eagerly participate in dressing up, making noise during the Megillah reading, and enjoying the festive atmosphere. The holiday provides an engaging way for them to connect with Jewish history and values.

## Part 5: Global Traditions

*"What about Jews in other countries? Do they eat those triangular cookies too?"*

Jewish communities around the world have developed unique customs and culinary traditions for Purim, reflecting their local cultures while maintaining the core themes of the holiday.

*   **Ashkenazi Jews:** The most widely recognized Ashkenazi customs include the consumption of **Hamantaschen** (Yiddish for "Haman's pockets" or "Haman's ears"), three-cornered pastries typically filled with poppy seeds, fruit preserves, or chocolate. Other traditional foods include **Kreplach** (meat-filled dumplings) and **Fassenacht** (fried doughnuts), especially in German-speaking communities. In some communities, long challah loaves called *koilitch*, twisted to evoke Haman's demise, are eaten.
*   **Sephardic Jews:** Many Sephardic communities also enjoy special pastries. Moroccan Jews bake challahs called **_Ojos de Haman_** ("Haman's eyes"), which include almonds and hard-boiled eggs. Some Rhodian Jewish communities prepare *folares*, pastries with a hard-boiled egg encased in challah dough, representing Haman's head. In Salonika, a custom involved throwing nuts at each other.
*   **Persian Jews:** Persian Jews historically made **_Nan-e Berenji_** (rice cookies) and **_Shirini-e Nokhodi_** (chickpea cookies). A playful tradition involved children climbing onto neighbors' roofs and lowering empty baskets down chimneys to be filled with fruit.
*   **Yemenite Jews:** Yemenite Jews have a tradition of creating a scarecrow-like effigy of Haman, which children would lead around town, receiving treats. They also had a custom of putting "Haman crosses" made of sticks, which were later burned.
*   **Iraqi Jews:** Iraqi Jews are known for making **_sambusak_**, a savory pastry filled with cheese, for Purim.
*   **Bukharan Jews:** In wintry Bukhara, a custom involved building a large "snow-Haman" near the synagogue, which would then be melted in a bonfire after the Purim feast, symbolizing Haman's demise.
*   **Ethiopian Jews (Beta Israel):** Historically, the Beta Israel community did not celebrate Purim as a major holiday, as it is not explicitly mentioned in the Torah. However, with increased integration into broader Jewish communities, some have adopted Purim customs.

## Tales

### The Humiliation of Haman

The Megillah recounts how King Ahashverosh, unable to sleep, reads the royal chronicles and discovers that Mordechai was never rewarded for uncovering a plot against the king. He asks Haman, who has just arrived to request permission to hang Mordechai, what should be done for a man the king wishes to honor. Haman, assuming the king wishes to honor him, suggests a grand procession where the man is dressed in royal robes, ridden on the king's horse, and led through the city by a noble. To Haman's horror, the king commands him to do all this for Mordechai.

Midrashic tradition adds layers of humiliation to this event. It is said that Haman once sold himself as a slave to Mordechai during a time of famine. When Haman was forced to lead Mordechai, Mordechai reminded him of his slave status. Furthermore, some Midrashim describe Haman having to act as Mordechai's barber before the procession, and even Mordechai kicking him as he mounted the horse, adding to Haman's utter disgrace. This tale emphasizes the dramatic reversal of fortune and the poetic justice meted out to the wicked Haman.

### The Children of Israel and Haman

Midrashim highlight the crucial role of Jewish children in the Purim miracle. It is said that Haman once found Mordechai teaching Torah to 22,000 children, who had been fasting for three days. Haman, intending to kill them first, ordered them shackled. However, their cries reached heaven, and God heard their prayers, leading to the tearing up of the decree against the Jews. Another Midrash relates that it was a group of children who first gave Mordechai an omen that his prayers would be answered, demonstrating their pure faith and the power of their innocence. This tale underscores the idea that the prayers and purity of even the youngest members of the community can bring about salvation.
