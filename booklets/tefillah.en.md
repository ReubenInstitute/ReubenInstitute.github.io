## Introduction

The Essence of Jewish Prayer

## Part 1: Origins

The origins of Jewish prayer are deeply rooted in the earliest narratives of the Hebrew Bible, reflecting a profound and often spontaneous connection between individuals and the Divine. In its nascent forms, prayer was not a fixed liturgy but a direct, heartfelt cry to God, born out of immediate need, distress, or profound spiritual yearning.

One of the earliest and most poignant examples of intercessory prayer comes from Moses. When his sister Miriam is afflicted with leprosy, Moses, despite her recent challenge to his authority, immediately turns to God with a concise and urgent plea for her healing.

> _"So Moses cried out to the Lord, saying, “O God, pray heal her!”"_ —Numbers 12:13

The story of Hannah reveals a woman in deep anguish, pouring out her soul before God in a desperate petition for a child.

> _"In her wretchedness, she prayed to יהוה, weeping all the while. And she made this vow: “O Lord of Hosts, if You will look upon the suffering of Your maidservant and will remember me and not forget Your maidservant, and if You will grant Your maidservant a male child, I will dedicate him to יהוה for all the days of his life; and no razor shall ever touch his head.”"_ —Shmuel Aleph 10-11

As the biblical narrative progresses, prayer also takes on a more public and declarative role, often in moments of national significance or spiritual confrontation. The prophet Elijah's prayer on Mount Carmel, for instance, is a dramatic appeal to God to demonstrate His sovereignty before a wavering people.

> _"When the evening offering was due, the prophet Elijah came forward and said, “O Lord, God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, today let it be known that You are God in Israel and that I am Your servant, and that I have done all these things at Your bidding. Answer me, יהוה, answer me, that this people may know that You, Lord, are God—for You have turned their hearts backward.”"_ —Melachim Aleph 18:36-37

Daniel's prayer, made with fasting, sackcloth, and ashes, is a comprehensive confession of the sins of his people and a fervent supplication for divine mercy and the fulfillment of God's promises.

> _"I turned my face to the Lord God to beg with prayer and supplications, with fasting and sackcloth and ashes. I prayed to יהוה my God, making confession and saying: “O Lord! Great and awesome God, who stays faithful to His covenant and kindness with those who love Him and keep His commandments!"_ —Daniel 9:3-4



## Part 2: Psalms

The Book of Psalms, referred to as the prayer book of ancient Israel, contains a vast collection of prayers that express the full spectrum of human emotion and experience.

The Psalms encompass a diverse range of literary forms and themes including laments, cries from individuals experiencing profound suffering seeking solace and intervention from God. There are hymns or songs of praise expressing adoration and worship for God's work in creation and history, thanksgiving Psalms recounting blessings and acts of kindness giving thanks for divine faithfulness and deliverance from distress, royal Psalms centered around the king celebrating coronations battles or divine protection often pointing to the ultimate kingship of God, wisdom Psalms imparting practical insights and moral teachings guiding believers toward a righteous life, pilgrimage Psalms Songs of Ascents sung by pilgrims on their way to Jerusalem, imprecatory Psalms calling on God to execute justice and judgment on enemies, and penitential Psalms expressing remorse and seeking God's forgiveness and mercy.

#### Authors

According to tradition, the Psalms were composed by various authors over a long period. King David is widely regarded as the primary author, with 73 psalms specifically attributed to him in their Hebrew titles, other traditional authors including Moses, Asaph (who has 12 psalms attributed to him), the sons of Korah (11 psalms), and Solomon. Jewish tradition also attributes some psalms to Adam, Melchizedek, and Abraham.

#### Styles

**Maskil**: This term, found in the titles of several psalms (e.g., Psalm 32), is generally understood to mean "a well-written song" or "contemplative." It suggests a psalm intended to impart wisdom or instruction, encouraging reflection and understanding of spiritual truths.

**Miktam**: Appearing in the titles of six psalms (e.g., Psalm 16, 56-60), the exact meaning of Miktam is uncertain, but it is often associated with a "golden" or "precious" composition, or perhaps a "mystery" or "secret." Some scholars suggest it may relate to a word meaning "to cover" or "expiation," implying a psalm intended to cover or atone for sin, often composed during times of distress or danger, reflecting deep trust in God.

**Shiggaion**: This term, found in Psalm 7 and Habakkuk 3:1, is believed to denote a "wild, mournful ode" or a "lyrical poem composed under strong mental emotion." Derived from a Hebrew root meaning "to wander" or "to reel," it suggests a song characterized by an erratic or passionate style, expressing deep personal fervor, urgency, or distress.

#### Performance

**Al-taschith**: This Hebrew phrase, meaning "Do Not Destroy," is found in the titles of Psalms 57, 58, 59, and 75. It is widely believed to be a musical or liturgical directive, possibly indicating a specific tune to which these psalms were to be sung, or a plea for divine preservation and protection, reflecting the psalmist's reliance on God during times of distress.

**Mahalath**: Appearing in the titles of Psalm 53 and Psalm 88, Mahalath is likely a musical term referring to a specific tune or style of playing. While its precise meaning is uncertain, some interpretations suggest it could relate to a "lute" or "guitar," or even convey a gloomy or sad tone, aligning with the somber content of these psalms.

**With stringed instruments**: Many psalms were intended to be accompanied by stringed instruments, which were central to Temple worship. This instruction indicates that the psalm was to be performed with instruments like harps and lyres, emphasizing their role in creating a rich musical backdrop for praise and worship.

**Upon Nehiloth**: This term, found in the title of Psalm 5, is believed to refer to wind instruments or flutes. It indicates that the psalm was to be performed with such instruments, suggesting a specific musical arrangement or mood.

**According to Gittith**: Appearing in the titles of Psalms 8, 81, and 84, "Gittith" is thought to refer to a musical instrument, a specific tune, or a musical style, possibly originating from the Philistine city of Gath or related to wine presses and vintage celebrations, implying a joyous character. It suggests a particular musical mode or melody to which these psalms were to be sung.

**Sheminith**: Found in the titles of Psalms 6 and 12, "Sheminith" literally means "the eighth." It is widely interpreted as a musical term indicating a lower octave, a bass register, or an eight-stringed instrument, possibly intended for male voices or a specific musical tuning.

**To the choirmaster / For the Chief Musician**: This common superscription, found in many psalms, indicates that the psalm was intended for the leader of the Temple music or choir director. It signifies instructions on how the psalm was to be sung or performed, highlighting the organized and intentional nature of worship music in ancient Israel. Some interpretations also suggest a deeper meaning, potentially referring to the Messiah.

**Selah**: Appearing 74 times in the Hebrew Bible, primarily in the Psalms, "Selah" is a word of uncertain etymology and precise meaning. It is widely believed to be a liturgical-musical mark, instructing a pause for reflection, a musical interlude, or a signal to "lift up" in praise, emphasizing the preceding text.

### Instruments

**Shofar**, a ram's horn, was not a melodic instrument but was blown to signal important events, such as war, festal occasions, or the presence of God. The Shofar's sound is often associated with praise and the celebration of God's kingship. The shofar was also used to announce the new moon and the Jubilee year.

> _"Praise him with **Shofar** sound."_ —Psalm 150:3

The Shofar is made from the horn of a kosher animal, most commonly a ram, but also goats or kudu. The horn is cleaned, hollowed, and polished, with the tip cut off to form a mouthpiece. Its natural curvature is preserved, giving it a characteristic bent or spiral shape, though some can be straightened. It has no valves or finger holes, and its sound is produced by the player's lips vibrating against the mouthpiece.

---

**Ḥatzotzrot** were trumpets used in ways similar to the Shofar, primarily by priests.

> _"With **Ḥatzotzrot** and the sound of the Shofar, shout for joy before the king, the Lord!"_ —Tehillim 98:6

But only the priests were permitted to blow the silver Ḥatzotzrot.

> _"Have two silver trumpets made; make them of hammered work. They shall serve you to summon [military bodies of] the community and to set the divisions in motion."_ —Bemidbar 10:2

Temple music featured Ḥatzotzrot, Divrei Hayamim mentioning one hundred and twenty priests each having one.

> _"All the Levites who were musicians—Asaph, Heman, Jeduthun and their sons and relatives—stood on the east side of the altar, dressed in fine linen and playing cymbals, harps and lyres. They were accompanied by 120 priests sounding trumpets."_ —Divrei Hayamim Bet 5:12

They were also used in significant events, such as David's processional band when transferring the Ark to Jerusalem, where seven musicians played seven Ḥatzotzrot:

> _"Shebaniah, Joshaphat, Nethanel, Amasai, Zechariah, Benaiah and Eliezer—the priests—were to blow the trumpets before the ark of God. ... _ —Divrei Hayamim Aleph 15:24

In Nechemya, priests are mentioned with Ḥatzotzrot.

> _"... as well as some priests with trumpets ..." —Nechemya 12:35

> _"... as well as the priests ... with their trumpets"_ — Nechemya 12:41


---

**Kinnor** was a prominent stringed instrument used in ancient Israelite worship, often played by the Levites in the Temple. The Kinnor was used for melodic accompaniment and was believed to have a "numinous effect," capable of driving away evil spirits and preparing for divine presence.

> _"Praise him with the **Kinnor** and **Nevel**."_ —Tehilim 150:3

The Kinnor was described by Josephus as having 10 strings, made from sheep's small intestine, and played with a plectrum or by hand. It likely consisted of a soundboard with two arms extending parallel to the body, connected by a yoke from which the strings ran down to the body. It is considered a type of yoke lute.

---

**Nevel**, similar to the Kinnor, was a stringed instrument frequently mentioned for accompanying worship.

> _"Sing praises to the Lord with the **Nevel**, with the **Nevel** and melodious music!"_ —Tehilim 98:5

The Nevel, a stringed instrument resembling the Kinnor but often larger and louder, typically had a hollow body or sound-chest, from which two raised arms extended, connected near the top by a crossbar or yoke. Strings were attached to this yoke.

---

**Tof** is a percussion instrument often associated with dancing and joyful celebration. Its inclusion signifies exuberant and physical expressions of praise.

> _"Praise him with **Tof** and dancing."_ —Tehilim 150:4

This percussion instrument consisted of a hoop of wood or metal over which an animal skin was stretched. Some versions included small, thin pieces of metal hung upon the rim, which jingled when shaken, similar to a modern tambourine. The instrument was held in one hand, and the drumhead was beaten with the fingers and the back of the other hand.

---

**Ugav** was a wind instrument mentioned in biblical texts, often alongside stringed instruments, contributing to joyful music and celebrations.

> _"praise him with the strings and **Ugav**."_ —Psalm 150:4

The Ugav is generally understood to be a type of pipe or flute, possibly a reed woodwind, pan pipes, an early form of modern organ. Some scholars suggest it was a hollow reed pipe with finger holes.

It is considered one of the earliest musical instruments, with Yuval, descendant of Cain, being described as the "father" of those playing the Ugav, traditionally the "father of music"

> _"And his brother's name was Yuval: he was the father of all such as handle the Harp and **Ugav**."_ —Bereshit 4:21

---

**Tziltzelim** were percussion instruments used to create loud, clashing sounds. They were employed to encourage listeners, signal important moments in worship, and express intense, joyful praise, symbolizing a call for all creation to join in the adoration of God.

> _"Praise him with the clash of **Tziltzelim**, praise him with resounding **Tziltzelim**."_ —Tehilim 150:5

These percussion instruments were typically two circular, concave disks made of metal, primarily bronze or copper. They had a domed center, often pierced for threading with a strap handle. Ancient Tziltzelim were smaller and thicker than modern ones, ranging from 3 to 12 cm in diameter. They were played by clashing them together.



## Part 3: Codification

The codification of Jewish prayer marks a significant transition from the spontaneous supplications of biblical figures to a more structured and communal liturgy, deeply rooted in rabbinic tradition and foundational texts. Even as prayer evolved, certain core expressions from the biblical period remained central to the developing liturgy. Among the most fundamental are the declaration of God's unity and the priestly blessing:

> _"Hear, O Israel: The Lord is our God, the Lord is One."_ —Deuteronomy 6:4

> _"The Lord bless you and keep you! The Lord deal kindly and graciously with you! The Lord bestow His favor upon you and grant you peace!"_ —Numbers 6:24-26

The formalization of Jewish prayer began in earnest during the Second Temple period, largely attributed to the Men of the Great Assembly (Anshei Knesset HaGedolah). This body of Sages, active during the early post-exilic era, played a crucial role in shaping Jewish law and practice, including the establishment of fixed prayers. Their foundational role is highlighted in rabbinic tradition:

> _"Moses received the Torah from Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua; and Joshua to the Elders; and the Elders to the Prophets; and the Prophets transmitted it to the Men of the Great Assembly. They [the Men of the Great Assembly] said three things: Be deliberate in judgment; develop many students; and make a fence for the Torah."_ —Pirkei Avot 1:1

The Men of the Great Assembly are specifically credited with instituting the framework for many of the blessings and prayers that form the core of Jewish liturgy, most notably the Amidah (the central standing prayer):

> _"Rabbi Chiyya bar Abba said that Rabbi Yochanan said: The members of the Great Assembly established for Israel blessings and prayers, sanctifications and havdalot."_ —Talmud Bavli, Berachot 33a (also cited in Jerusalem Talmud, Berachot 2:4)

This shift towards fixed prayer introduced discussions among the Sages regarding the balance between spontaneous devotion and prescribed recitation. While fixed prayers provide structure and ensure communal participation, there was also an emphasis on maintaining genuine intention (kavanah):

> _"Rabbi Eliezer says: One whose prayer is fixed, his prayer is not supplication and is flawed."_ —Mishnah Berachot 4:4

Beyond the content, the codification also addressed the practical aspects of prayer, including specific times for their recitation. For instance, the Mishnah details the precise periods for reciting the Shema:

> _"From when, that is, from what time, does one recite Shema in the evening? From the time when the priests enter to partake of their teruma. Until when does the time for the recitation of the evening Shema extend? Until the end of the first watch, the words of Rabbi Eliezer. And the Rabbis say: The time for the recitation of the evening Shema is until midnight. Rabban Gamliel says: One may recite Shema until dawn."_ —Mishnah Berachot 1:1

Furthermore, the Sages emphasized the importance of a serious and focused mindset when engaging in prayer, particularly for the Amidah, underscoring that even within a fixed liturgy, personal devotion is paramount:

> _"One starts to pray only in a serious frame of mind. The early Hasidim waited one hour before praying in order to be able to concentrate. Even if the king greets one, he must not respond. Even if a snake is wound around one's heel, he must not interrupt."_ —Mishnah Berachot 5:1

The Talmud Bavli, particularly tractate Berachot, serves as a foundational text of Jewish law and thought, extensively elaborating on the laws and concepts of prayer, including the Shema and the Amidah, their timings, intentions, and various related halakhic discussions, thereby solidifying the codified practices for generations to come.

## Part 4: Evolution

The evolution of Jewish prayer from its spontaneous biblical expressions to its contemporary codified forms is a rich tapestry woven from historical events, geographical dispersion, and profound spiritual developments. This dynamic process has led to the emergence of standardized prayer books, the diversification of liturgical traditions (*Nusachim*), and unique adaptations shaped by the experiences of communities across the globe.

### Siddurim

Initially, Jewish prayer was largely an oral tradition, with individuals or communities offering heartfelt supplications or reciting scriptural passages from memory. The earliest components of Jewish prayer, such as the *Shema Yisrael* and the Priestly Blessing, are found within the Torah itself. As the Jewish people spread and the need for a more structured and communal liturgy grew, the process of codification began. The Men of the Great Assembly, active during the early post-exilic era, played a foundational role in establishing the framework for many blessings and prayers, most notably the *Amidah*.

The first known comprehensive compilation of Jewish prayers into a dedicated book, often referred to as a *siddur* (from the Hebrew root meaning 'order'), is attributed to Rav Amram Gaon, head of the Sura Academy in Babylonia, around 850 CE. His "Seder Rav Amram" was compiled in response to a request from the Jews of Spain and became a foundational text, influencing nearly all subsequent prayer books. Less than a century later, Saadia Gaon, also from Sura, composed his own *siddur*, which included explanations and instructions in Judeo-Arabic to serve communities in Arab lands. Maimonides (Rambam) further contributed to this formalization by outlining the order of prayers in his monumental legal code, the *Mishneh Torah*, which profoundly influenced Yemenite liturgy. Another significant early compilation was the 11th-century *Machzor Vitry*, based on the teachings of Rashi.

The invention of the printing press in the 15th century revolutionized the accessibility and standardization of Jewish prayer. The first printed *siddur*, *Minhag Romi*, was produced by the Soncino family in Italy in 1486. This technological advancement made prayer books widely available to the general public, fostering greater congregational participation and facilitating the widespread adoption of new prayers, including those influenced by Kabbalah. Vernacular translations of the *siddur* also began to appear, further broadening access to prayer. Today, while maintaining a core structure, new *siddurim* continue to be compiled, reflecting the ongoing adaptation of Jewish prayer to contemporary needs.

### Nusachim

The term *Nusach* (plural *Nusachim*) refers to the specific style, chosen hymns, tunes, and wording of Jewish prayers, encompassing the entire liturgical tradition of a community. The dispersion of Jewish communities across different regions following various exiles led to the development of distinct *Nusachim*. While core prayers like the *Amidah* and *Shema* generally maintain similar structures, more significant variations emerged in later additions, particularly in *piyyutim* (liturgical poems) and supplemental prayers.

Major *Nusachim* include Ashkenazi (Central and Eastern European), Sephardic (Iberian and North African), Mizrahi (Middle Eastern), Italian, and Yemenite. Despite these variations, a remarkable degree of textual commonality persists, with most *Nusachim* sharing approximately 90% of their content. Jewish tradition often emphasizes the importance of individuals continuing their family's customary *Nusach*.

A prime example of a distinct and ancient tradition is found among **Yemenite Jews**, whose *Nusach* is heavily influenced by Maimonides' codification in his *Mishneh Torah*. Within the Yemenite community, two primary *Nusachim* developed: **Baladi**, which adheres closely to the older, traditional Yemenite practice and Maimonides' rulings, often being shorter in length; and **Shami**, which emerged later, incorporating elements from Sephardic *Siddurim* and Kabbalistic concepts, particularly those derived from the AriZal, influenced by figures like Rabbi Shalom Sharabi. Distinctions in Yemenite prayer also extend to the pronunciation of Hebrew letters and vowels, further setting their tradition apart.

Within the broader Ashkenazi *Nusach*, distinct regional variations also emerged, notably those associated with **Lithuania (*Lita*) and Poland (*Polin*)**. *Nusach Lita* generally refers to the prayer tradition of non-Hasidic Ashkenazi Jews, particularly those from Lithuania (often called Litvaks). In contrast, *Nusach Polin* often refers to the Hasidic rite, which adopted *Nusach Sefard* due to the significant Hasidic presence in Poland. *Nusach Sefard* itself is a hybrid, incorporating Sephardic customs and Kabbalistic meditations (*kavanot*) from the teachings of Rabbi Isaac Luria (the Ari HaKadosh), despite its adherents often having Ashkenazi origins. Differences between *Nusach Lita* and *Nusach Polin* can be found in specific wordings, the order of prayers, and the inclusion or exclusion of certain *piyyutim*. Due to demographic shifts, contemporary mainstream Ashkenazi practice often aligns more closely with *Minhag Polin*.

### Inquisition

The Spanish Inquisition and the expulsions from Spain (1492) and Portugal (1497) forced many Jews, known as Conversos or Crypto-Jews, to outwardly adopt Catholicism while secretly maintaining their Jewish faith. This period also saw extensive efforts by Christian authorities to censor Jewish religious texts. Driven by accusations that works like the Talmud contained blasphemous references to Jesus or Christianity, censors systematically removed, altered, or even burned Hebrew books.

#### Scriptures

Passages in the Talmud mentioning a figure named "Yeshu" (a common Hebrew name, but often interpreted by Christian authorities as referring to Jesus of Nazareth) were frequently targeted for removal. One clear example of this selective censorship, where the narrative remains but the explicit identification is removed, can be found in Talmud Bavli, tractate Sanhedrin (with a parallel in Sotah 47a).

This passage tells a story about Rabbi Yehoshua ben Perachya and his student. In uncensored manuscripts, this student is explicitly identified as "Yeshu HaNotzri" (Jesus the Nazarene).

> _"Our Rabbis taught: Let the left hand repulse but the right hand always invite back: not as Elisha, who thrust Gehazi away with both hands, and not like R. Joshua b. Peraḥyah, who repulsed **Yeshu the Notzri** with both hands."_ -Sanhedrin 107b

Another example is found in the uncensored Munich Talmud manuscript, which preserves a passage that was censored out of many printed editions. It states:

> _"On the Eve of Passover they hung Yeshu the Notzri. And the herald went out before him for forty days [saying]: 'Yeshu the Notzri will go out to be stoned for sorcery and misleading and enticing Israel [to idolatry]. Any who knows [anything] in his defence must come and declare concerning him.' But no one came to his defence so they hung him on the Eve of Passover."_ -Tractate Sanhedrin 43a

#### Siddurim

Censorship significantly impacted the evolution and diversification of Jewish liturgical traditions (Nusachim). A prime example of this is the **Birkat HaMinim** ("Blessing on the Heretics"), a prayer within the **Amidah** (the central standing prayer).

The Amidah originally consisted of 18 blessings. However, the current weekday Amidah typically has 19 blessings. This discrepancy stems from the later addition of a blessing against heretics (Birkat HaMinim). The Talmud records the origin of this 19th blessing. Following the destruction of the Second Temple, Rabban Gamliel, recognizing the need to address the threats posed by various groups, including heretics and informers, sought someone to compose a new blessing. The Talmud states:

> "Rabban Gamliel said to the Sages: 'Is there no one who knows how to establish a prayer against the heretics?' Shmuel HaKatan stood up and established it." -Talmud Bavli, Berakhot 28b

This account highlights the deliberate addition of the Birkat HaMinim to the Amidah, reflecting the rabbinic leadership's response to internal and external challenges facing the Jewish community in the post-Temple era.

The oldest version of the Birkat HaMinim, as found in Cairo Genizah fragments and Maimonides' own manuscripts, says:

> "For the **Meshumadim** let there be no hope. And let the **Memshelet Zadon** be speedily uprooted in our days. Let the **Nozerim** and the **Minim** be destroyed in a moment."

This blessing was originally formulated to address various threats to the Jewish community, including early Jewish-Christian sects. The text reflects the prayer's early intent to curse those perceived as threats:

*   **"Memshelet Zadon" (arrogant government)** was primarily understood to refer to the **Roman Empire**, which had destroyed the Second Temple and oppressed the Jewish people.
*   **"Minim" (heretics)** referred to Jewish groups deemed heretical by the rabbinic establishment, such as early Jewish-Christian sects.
*   **"Meshumadim" (apostates)** specifically targeted Jews who had abandoned Judaism, particularly those who converted to other religions, including Christianity.
*   **"Notzrim" (Nazarenes/Christians)** explicitly referred to **Christians**. While "Minim" could sometimes include Jewish Christians, "Notzrim" was the direct term for followers of Jesus, particularly as Christianity developed into a distinct religion.

From the 13th century onwards, as Christian authorities became aware of its content, the Birkat HaMinim underwent extensive censorship in Jewish communities living under Christian rule.

The **Ashkenazi** and **Sephardic** versions show that under pressure from Christian censors, direct references were removed or replaced. A common Ashkenazi version reads:

> "For the **Malshinim** let there be no hope, and may all the evil in an instant be destroyed and all Your enemies be cut down swiftly; and the evil ones uproot and break and destroy and humble soon in our days."

In this version, the explicit terms **"Notzrim"** and **"Minim"** were removed. **"Meshumadim"** was modified to **"Malshinim"** (slanderers/informers), a broader term that could apply to various adversaries without explicitly naming Christians.

The modern **Yemenite** version, while rooted in ancient tradition, often reflects the historical removal of explicit mentions of "Notzrim" and sometimes "Minim" due to censorship and evolving sensibilities. A concise form, similar to a Jerusalem Talmud version, illustrates this:

> "For the **Meshumadim** let there be no hope, and uproot the **Memshelet Zadon**, speedily and in our days."

 In this modern Yemenite version, the terms **"Notzrim"** and **"Minim"** were removed from the direct curse, while **"Meshumadim"** and **"Memshelet Zadon"** (arrogant government) survived the censorship.

Jewish communities in more isolated regions, such as Yemen, preserved older, less censored versions of the Birkat HaMinim due to their relative detachment from these direct pressures. This highlights how external forces significantly shaped the liturgical texts of Jewish communities in Europe, while those in more secluded areas maintained forms closer to the original formulations, which were rooted in earlier Talmudic discussions about confronting perceived heresy.
