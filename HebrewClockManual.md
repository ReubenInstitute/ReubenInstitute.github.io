# Hebrew Clock Manual

## Scholarly

### The Hebrew Date

A Hebrew date begins around sunset, and runs through the night into the daylight that follows, ending again around the next sunset. The night comes first, then the daylight that follows it — evening and morning together make one Hebrew date.

### Nightfall and dawn

Two moments are used in halachic calculations: dawn and nightfall. Dawn is the point before sunrise when the sky begins to lighten. Nightfall is the point after sunset when the sky finishes darkening.

### Gra vs. Magen Avraham

There is a dispute about where the halachic day begins and ends: at sunrise and sunset, or at dawn and nightfall.

The Vilna Gaon, Rabbi Eliyahu of Vilna, held that the day runs from sunrise to sunset.

The Magen Avraham, Rabbi Avraham Gombiner, held that the day runs from dawn to nightfall.

### Seasonal hours

The day is divided into twelve seasonal hours, from its start to its end. Each seasonal hour is a twelfth of the day's length, so it changes with the seasons, longer in summer, shorter in winter. The night is counted in watches, either three or four, falling on exact seasonal hours.

### Zmanim

Zmanim are the specific times of day that mark halachic obligations, primarily the daily prayers.

Dawn is the first light, before sunrise.

Misheyakir is the earliest time to don tallit and tefillin, after dawn.

Sunrise is when the sun's disc begins to appear.

Shema is the latest time to recite the morning Shema.

Amidah is the latest time to recite the morning Amidah.

Minchah Gedolah is the earliest time for the afternoon Minchah prayer.

Minchah Ketanah is the preferred time for Minchah.

Plag Minchah is the latest time for Minchah.

Sunset is when the sun's disc completely disappears.

Nightfall is the point after sunset when the sky finishes darkening.

### Opinions

#### Dawn

16.1° — the Magen Avraham holds that dawn is 72 minutes before sunrise. At Jerusalem's latitude, on an equinox, the sun is 16.1 degrees below the horizon at that moment.

16.9° — the Gra and the Baal HaTanya hold that dawn is 72 minutes before sunrise. The Baal HaTanya measures sunrise itself as the moment the sun is 1.583 degrees below the horizon. At Jerusalem's latitude, on an equinox, the sun reaches 16.9 degrees below the horizon 72 minutes before that moment.

18° — astronomical dawn, the general threshold at which light first becomes detectable, when the sun is 18 degrees below the horizon.

19.8° — the Geonim hold that a mil takes 22.5 minutes to walk, so dawn, four mil before sunrise, is 90 minutes before sunrise. At Jerusalem's latitude, on an equinox, the sun is 19.8 degrees below the horizon at that moment.

72 minutes — Rabbeinu Tam holds that a mil takes 18 minutes to walk, so dawn, four mil before sunrise, is 72 minutes before sunrise. He takes this as 72 fixed clock minutes.

72 relative minutes — the same opinion, that dawn is 72 minutes before sunrise, but understood as 72 seasonal minutes rather than fixed clock minutes.


#### Nightfall

6° — the Baal HaTanya holds that nightfall is at this angle below the horizon. It is his standard tzait, used for zman krias Shema and similar everyday matters.

5.833° — a lenient Chabad opinion, used for matters of rabbinic (derabanan) obligation.

5.95° —

6.3° — a Chabad opinion calculated by Rabbi Avrohom Altein of Chabad of Winnipeg.

6.833° — a stringent Chabad opinion, used for matters of Torah (deoraita) obligation.

7.1° —

8° —

8.5° — the Baal HaTanya holds that nightfall for Motzai Shabbat, Motzai Yom Kippur, and Motzai Yom Tov is at this stricter angle, later than his standard 6°.

16.1° —

18° — astronomical dusk, the general threshold at which light finally disappears, when the sun is 18 degrees below the horizon.

19.8° — the Magen Avraham holds that nightfall is 90 minutes after sunset, mirroring his dawn opinion. At Jerusalem's latitude, on an equinox, the sun is 19.8 degrees below the horizon at that moment.

72 minutes — Rabbeinu Tam holds that nightfall is 72 minutes after sunset, the same 4-mil reckoning as his dawn opinion, taken as 72 fixed clock minutes.

72 relative minutes — the same opinion, understood as 72 seasonal minutes rather than fixed clock minutes.

90 relative minutes — the Magen Avraham's 90-minute nightfall, understood as 90 seasonal minutes rather than fixed clock minutes.

### Academic View

The Academic View maps the complete seasonal Hebrew day onto the dial, with midnight (Chatzot HaLayla) at the bottom and midday (Chatzot HaYom) at the top. Sunrise sits on the left, sunset on the right, and the night arc fills the lower half of the face. Four major marks indicate the day's cardinal moments — sunset, midnight, sunrise, and midday. Between them, eight smaller hour marks divide the day and night, with twelve fine half‑hour marks providing an additional layer of precision.
